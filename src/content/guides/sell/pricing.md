## TL;DR

- Price against **the cost of the alternative**, not against your costs and not against competitors you do not have. The alternative is never nothing — it is a spreadsheet plus someone's Tuesday, an agency retainer, or a tolerated loss.
- First prices are **systematically too low**, and the cause is not analysis. Founders price against their own willingness to pay, and a low price reduces the chance of hearing no.
- Find the **approval threshold** before you pick a number. A price under a sign-off limit closes in days; a price above it acquires a procurement process and six weeks. The two numbers are often very close.
- **One flat price for one package**, at first. It removes a negotiation dimension, removes several payment states from your software, and makes the single number you are testing legible.
- There is **no credible published benchmark** for what a first product should cost. Any number you have seen is from a sample of companies with brands, salespeople and comparison sets. Treat the absence honestly rather than borrowing a figure.
- Raise on **new customers first**, grandfather existing ones explicitly. If the new number closes at the same rate, the old one was too low.

## Who this is for

You have something people might pay for and no idea what to charge. There is no established market price because the thing is new, or because the people who do something like it do it as a service, or because the incumbent is a spreadsheet. You are about to pick a number mostly by feel.

Skip this if you are in a market with published list prices and your question is where to sit within a known range — that is positioning, and it is a much easier problem with far more available evidence. Skip it too if your question is whether to charge at all: charge. [Founder-led sales](/sell/) covers why the free-to-paid transition is where you find out whether the problem is real.

## The problem

Pricing with no comparables fails in a specific direction. Not randomly — **downward**, nearly always, and for three reasons that compound.

**You price against your own wallet.** Founders are usually not their own buyer, and they are usually poorer than their buyer. You imagine spending $500 a month of your own money and flinch. The operations director with a £40,000 software line and a problem costing her two days a month does not flinch, and she is the person paying. This substitution is almost invisible from the inside, because the flinch feels like judgement.

**A low price is insurance against rejection.** Same mechanism as over-scoping a first version, pointed at the price tag. A low number reduces the chance of hearing no, and in the moment that feels like commercial prudence. What it actually buys is a worse signal: a yes at a trivially low price tells you very little about whether the problem is worth solving.

**You price the software rather than the outcome.** Your mental model is a product with features, so you benchmark against other products with features. The buyer's model is a problem with a cost. Those produce different numbers, and the buyer's is usually larger.

The consequences are asymmetric, which is why this matters more than it seems. Too high, and you lose deals, hear why, and lower it — a week's correction. Too low, and three bad things happen at once and all are slow to fix. Your unit economics are wrong from the start, and [CB Insights' March 2026 analysis of 431 failed VC-backed companies](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/) found unsustainable unit economics cited in **19%** of shutdowns. You attract the least committed segment, who churn and complain most. And you have set an anchor with customers who will be your references, so raising it is a negotiation with the people whose goodwill you most depend on.

A low price is also read as a claim. A product at $9 a month is asserting that the problem it solves is worth about $9 a month, and buyers with expensive problems believe you.

There is a fourth problem, which is the reason this guide exists in its current shape: **you will go looking for a benchmark and find figures that do not apply to you.** The published pricing research is drawn from companies with brands, sales teams, comparison sets and years of data. There is no credible published answer to "what should a first product from an unknown company cost," and the honest move is to say so rather than borrow a number whose sample is nothing like you.

## What to price against

Four candidate anchors. Only the first two are any use early.

| Anchor | How to get the number | Worth using? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The labour the status quo costs** | Ask: who does it, how long does it take, how often. Multiply by a loaded hourly cost. | **Yes, best.** It is specific, the buyer supplies it, and it is a comparison they can make without you. |
| **What they already pay to make it less bad** | Ask: what do you spend on this today — tools, agency, contractor, overtime, write-offs. | **Yes.** An existing budget line is far stronger evidence than a stated intention. |
| **Competitor list prices** | Public pricing pages. | **Rarely.** If you had real comparables you would not be reading this. Adjacent products anchor the buyer wrongly and drag you toward their price metric. |
| **Your costs plus a margin** | Infrastructure, your time. | **No.** Irrelevant to the buyer, and at startup scale it produces absurdly low numbers because your costs are tiny and your value may not be. |

The method is one question asked properly, and it is the same question that qualifies the deal: **what does this cost you today, in money or hours?** Ask, then be silent. Do not supply a number — a buyer who agrees with your estimate has been polite, not informative, and [discovery calls](/sell/discovery-and-demos/) covers why that politeness quietly corrupts a forecast.

Then price at a fraction of that number, where the fraction is visible to the buyer. If the status quo costs them £1,800 a month in someone's time, £400 a month is an easy comparison they can make in their head and defend to their boss. £1,500 is a negotiation about whether your estimate was right.

On survey methods: the best-known is van Westendorp's Price Sensitivity Meter, four open-ended questions proposed at the 1976 ESOMAR congress. It is worth knowing it exists and worth not using it at ten customers. Van Westendorp himself noted that there was not yet a well-developed theory behind it, and it measures *stated perceptions* of hypothetical prices, which is a weaker instrument than the real budget a buyer already spends. Save it for when you have enough traffic that stated preference is the only data available at scale.

## Picking the actual number

Three inputs, in this order.

**1. The fraction of the alternative's cost.** From above. This gives you a range rather than a number — usually something like a fifth to a third of what the status quo costs them.

**2. The approval threshold.** This is the input founders miss and it frequently matters more than the first. Every company has sign-off limits: a manager can approve up to X, above that it goes to a director, above that to finance, and somewhere there is a line that triggers procurement, security review and a legal redline. Those limits are **discontinuities**, not gradients. $4,800 a year and $5,200 a year are not 8% apart in difficulty; one closes in a week and the other adds a quarter.

You find out by asking, on the first call: *how did the last tool like this get bought here, and what's the sign-off limit?* It is a neutral logistics question and people answer it plainly.

**3. Roundness and confidence.** Pick a round number and say it without apology or qualification. "It's £400 a month." Not "it would normally be around four, but for you…". The delivery is part of the price: a hedged number invites negotiation and signals that the first figure was not real.

A practical heuristic for the first ten, worth more than any model: **pick the number that makes you slightly uncomfortable to say out loud, then say it without flinching.** The discomfort is the signal that you have stopped pricing against your own wallet. If nobody pushes back at all, it was too low — a complete absence of price objection across ten deals is itself information.

## The price metric

Separate from the number: what are you charging *per*?

Early, charge **one flat price for one package**. Per month or per year, same price for everyone, one thing included. Three reasons.

It removes a dimension of negotiation. Per-seat invites "but only three of us would really use it," which is a conversation about your pricing model rather than about their problem.

It removes work from your software. Every pricing dimension multiplies the states your system has to handle — proration, mid-cycle changes, overages, downgrades — and each of those is a path that is wrong by default. [Shipping without engineers](/build/shipping-without-engineers/) covers why payment states are among the first things to break for founder-built software.

It makes the experiment legible. You are testing one number. Two dimensions means two variables and ten data points, which tests nothing.

Add a dimension only when customers' own descriptions of the value point at it consistently. If three buyers independently say "this saves us a day per store," per-store is your metric. Do not pick a metric from a framework; take it from the noun your buyers already use when they describe the value.

## Raising it

You will price too low. Here is the cheap correction.

**New customers first.** Change the price for the next cohort. Keep existing customers where they are. This is free information: if the new number closes at a comparable rate, the old one was too low, and you have learned it without risking a single reference.

**Grandfather explicitly, with an end date and a list price.** Not "we'll keep you at this rate," which creates an argument with no scheduled time. Instead: "your founding rate is £200 a month for 24 months. List is £500. After that we'll talk." Now the increase is a diary entry rather than a betrayal.

**Discount the price, never the promise.** A discount with the full promise intact gives you a reference customer who got a deal. A reduced scope at full price gives you a customer who believes they bought something else, and that gets discovered at the worst possible moment. Keep what you promised whole.

**When you do move existing customers:** long notice, a reason tied to what you have actually added since they signed, and an option to lock the old rate by committing to a longer term. Expect some churn. The churn is mostly concentrated in the segment that was only ever there for the price, which is the segment you were trying to stop attracting.

**Do not raise prices to fix a fundraising narrative.** It is a visible temptation when revenue multiples are the metric being discussed, and it produces a cohort of unhappy customers precisely when you need references. [Raising a first round](/run/fundraising/) covers what investors actually read, and a hurried price rise is legible to them as exactly what it is.

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. Numbers are invented to be plausible; no company described here exists.*

A founder building a tool that assembles regulatory documentation for small medical-device manufacturers. Nothing comparable exists as software — the work is done by consultants or internally by a regulatory affairs person.

**The first price.** She picks £49 a month, reasoning from her own sense of what software costs. Five customers sign in eight weeks. Nobody negotiates. Nobody asks for a discount. Not one buyer asks what it costs — several say "that's nothing, just put it on the card." That unanimous absence of friction is the clearest possible signal and she initially reads it as product-market fit.

**The cost question.** On the sixth call she asks properly what the status quo costs, and waits. The buyer works it out: they use a consultant at £900 a day, roughly fourteen days a year on documentation assembly, so about **£12,600 a year**, plus internal time nobody counts. Her product removes perhaps nine of those days.

So the alternative costs £12,600 and she is charging £588 a year. She is priced at under 5% of the cost of the thing she replaces, which is not a discount, it is a failure to be legible as a serious purchase.

**The approval threshold.** She starts asking how comparable tools get bought. The pattern across eleven conversations: at companies under 200 people, the quality or regulatory lead can approve up to **£5,000 a year** without finance involvement. Above that, finance, a security questionnaire, and typically six to ten weeks.

So there is a cliff just under £5,000.

**The new price.** £3,600 a year, billed annually, one package, everything included. Comfortably under the threshold, and roughly 29% of the consultant cost — a fraction the buyer can compute in their head and defend upward in one sentence. She picks £3,600 rather than £4,800 deliberately, leaving room to raise again without crossing the cliff.

Saying it is harder than setting it. The first three times she says "three thousand six hundred a year" she hears her own voice go up at the end. The fourth buyer asks what it includes, she answers, and they say fine.

**The results over the next quarter.** 14 new deals attempted, 6 closed. At £49 she was closing roughly 5 in 8. So the close rate fell from about 63% to about 43% — and revenue per deal went up **6.1x**. Six deals at £3,600 is £21,600 against what would have been fourteen attempts yielding maybe nine deals at £588, or £5,292. The lower close rate is not a problem; it is the price of selling to people with budgets.

The three objections she now hears, which she never heard at £49, are all useful: what happens if we stop paying and need the documents (a real product gap, now fixed), can we pay monthly (yes, at £360 a month, which is 20% more annually and most take it), and can you do a pilot (yes, one submission, £900, credited against the annual).

**The existing five.** She writes to them: founding rate of £49 a month held for 24 months, list price now £3,600 a year, and a specific offer — lock £1,800 a year for three years if they want to commit now. Two take the lock. Three stay on £49. None churn. One replies that they had assumed the low price meant the company would not survive, which she had not considered as a cost of underpricing and now mentions on calls.

**What she got wrong.** She raised the price and did not change the sales process, so for six weeks she was selling a £3,600 product with a £49 pitch — no case for the cost of the status quo, no reference to the consultant comparison. The three deals she lost in that window were all lost on "we'll think about it," which is the signature of a price that has not been justified rather than one that is too high. The fix was two sentences added to the call: what the consultant route costs, and what fraction of it this is.

## Checklist

Before quoting a number:

- [ ] Asked what the status quo costs in money or hours, and **waited** for their number rather than supplying one.
- [ ] Asked what they already spend on making it less bad — tools, agency, overtime, write-offs.
- [ ] Asked how the last comparable tool got bought here, and the sign-off limit.
- [ ] Chosen a number that is a visible fraction of the alternative's cost.
- [ ] Checked the number sits **under** the nearest approval threshold, with room to raise later.
- [ ] One flat price, one package, no seat or usage dimension yet.
- [ ] Round number, said in a short sentence with no hedge.
- [ ] Noticed whether the number makes me slightly uncomfortable. If not, it is probably too low.

Ongoing:

- [ ] Price objections tracked. **Zero objections across ten deals means the price is too low**, not that it is right.
- [ ] Discounts recorded as a named founding rate, with an end date and a stated list price.
- [ ] Every discount preserves the full promise; nothing is quietly removed from scope.
- [ ] A scheduled review of the price for new customers every quarter.
- [ ] Price metric revisited only when buyers' own language consistently points at one.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** Nobody ever objects to the price.
**Cause:** It is too low. A price at the edge of justifiable always draws some pushback.
**Fix:** Raise it for new customers and watch the close rate. A fall in close rate with a larger rise in revenue per deal is a win, not a warning.

**Symptom:** Customers churn quickly and complain disproportionately while paying the least.
**Cause:** A low price selected for the least committed segment.
**Fix:** Raise the price on new customers; the segment composition changes within a cohort or two.

**Symptom:** Every deal becomes a negotiation about the pricing model rather than the problem.
**Cause:** Too many pricing dimensions too early.
**Fix:** One flat price for one package. Re-introduce dimensions only when a buyer's own words name the metric.

**Symptom:** A deal that looked a week from closing acquired a procurement process.
**Cause:** The price crossed an approval threshold nobody asked about.
**Fix:** Ask the sign-off question on first calls. Where a small reduction lands you under a cliff, it is almost always worth taking.

**Symptom:** You raised the price and started losing deals on "we'll think about it."
**Cause:** The price moved and the justification did not. This is the most common failure after a rise.
**Fix:** Add the status-quo cost and the fraction comparison explicitly to the call. Same number, different conversation.

**Symptom:** Early customers are hostile at renewal.
**Cause:** A discount that was never named, dated, or given a list price, so the increase arrives as a surprise.
**Fix:** Name every discount at the time it is given. Retroactively, give very long notice and offer a lock-in rate.

**Symptom:** A customer believes they bought something you did not sell them.
**Cause:** The promise was discounted along with the price, informally, mid-call.
**Fix:** Write what is included in the email that confirms the deal, every time, in the buyer's words.
