## TL;DR

- Spend the first **two thirds** of the call not demoing. A thirty-minute call is roughly twenty minutes of questions, eight minutes of demo aimed at what they said, two minutes agreeing a next step.
- [Gong's analysis of 519,000 recorded discovery calls](https://www.gong.io/blog/deal-closing-discovery-call) associates success with **11 to 14 questions** and **3 to 4 problems** discussed. Both failure shapes are common: three questions and a demo, or thirty questions and no demo.
- Establish the **cost of doing nothing** before you say a price. A problem with no quantified cost has no budget behind it, and you want to know that in minute twelve rather than after four weeks of follow-up.
- **Demo only what answers a problem they named**, and open each section by quoting them back. Everything else you show dilutes the parts that mattered and invites objections about things that do not.
- End with a **dated next step and a named owner**, or an explicit no. "I'll send something over" is how a quarter disappears.

## Who this is for

You have calls booked and you are not sure what to do in them, or you are doing something and it is not converting. You have probably watched a professional demo and noticed yours does not feel like that, and concluded the problem is polish.

Skip this if you cannot get calls booked at all — that is [cold outbound](/sell/outbound/), and it is a different problem with a different fix. Skip it too if you are post-product-market-fit with a sales team; you need a qualification framework your reps can apply consistently, which is a heavier machine than this.

## The problem

Almost every bad founder sales call has the same structure, and it comes from a reasonable instinct.

The buyer agreed to a call about your product. So you show them your product. You share your screen four minutes in and spend twenty-five minutes walking through what it does, in roughly the order it was built, because that is the order you know it in. They ask a few questions. You answer them well. At the end they say it looks great and they will discuss it internally. You never hear from them again, and you cannot say why.

What went wrong is that the demo **answered questions nobody asked**. You showed eleven capabilities. Perhaps two were relevant to this buyer. You did not know which two, so you showed all eleven, and the nine irrelevant ones did active damage: they diluted attention, they consumed the time you needed for the real conversation, and every one of them was an invitation to object to something that does not matter. A buyer who spends four minutes on your permissions model — because you showed it, so they assume it matters — has spent four minutes not telling you what is actually breaking in their week.

The second failure is subtler and worse. The call produced no information. You know whether they liked the demo. You do not know what they do today, what it costs them, who else decides, or whether anything makes this urgent. So you cannot forecast the deal, you cannot write a follow-up that says anything specific, and — the part that compounds — you cannot use the call to improve the product or the pitch. [Founder-led sales](/sell/) makes the general argument: the first ten calls are research with a payment attached, and a demo-first call returns almost no research.

Why founders do it anyway: the demo is the part you control. Asking questions means sitting in silence, hearing that the problem is not very important, or being asked something you cannot answer. Demoing is performing, and performing is more comfortable than inquiring. That is the whole mechanism, and naming it is most of the fix.

## The shape of the call

Thirty minutes. If they offered an hour, still run thirty and give the time back — ending early with a clear next step reads as confidence and costs nothing.

**Minutes 0–2: frame it.** Say what you are going to do and get permission. "I'd like to spend most of this asking about how you handle X today, and then show you the part that's relevant rather than the whole thing. Does that work?" This single sentence buys you the twenty minutes of questions that would otherwise feel evasive. Almost nobody says no.

**Minutes 2–20: the questions.** Below. This is the call.

**Minutes 20–28: demo, narrow.** Only the parts that answer problems they named. Open each with a quote: "you said the hand-off to finance is where it stalls — here's that bit."

**Minutes 28–30: the next step.** One concrete action, a date, a named owner. Or a warm, clear no.

The ratio is the thing to hold on to. Founders drift toward demo because it fills time easily. If you are more than ten minutes in and already sharing your screen, the call is already the bad shape.

## The questions

Five categories. Gong's figure of 11 to 14 questions and 3 to 4 problems is a useful calibration rather than a script — the point is that a real discovery conversation has roughly this density, and both of the failure shapes are immediately recognisable against it.

| Category | What you are establishing | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The problem, in their words** | What is actually annoying, described the way an insider describes it. | How do you handle X today? Walk me through the last time it went wrong. What part of it do people complain about? |
| **The status quo** | What they use now, including spreadsheets, people and nothing. | What's in place today? Who does it? What did you try before this? Why did that stop? |
| **The cost of doing nothing** | Money or hours, with a number they say out loud. | Roughly how long does that take, and how often? What does it cost when it goes wrong? What do you pay today to make it less bad? |
| **Process and authority** | Who decides, who blocks, what the steps are. | Who else would need to be comfortable with this? How did the last tool like this get bought? Is there a budget this comes out of, or does one need to be made? |
| **The trigger** | Why now, and what happens if nothing happens. | What made you take this call? What changes in the next quarter? What happens if this stays as it is for a year? |

Three notes on using these.

**The status-quo question is the most underrated.** "What did you try before, and why did it stop?" tells you the competitive set, the buyer's sophistication, and the failure mode that will be used to judge you — all at once. And the answer "nothing, we just live with it" is the most important answer available, because it means you are not competing with a vendor, you are competing with inertia, which is harder and priced differently.

**The cost question must produce a number from them, not from you.** If you supply the number — "so that's probably costing you ten hours a week?" — you have given them permission to agree politely and you have learned nothing. Ask, then wait. The silence is uncomfortable and it is where the information is.

**The authority question is the one founders skip** because it feels presumptuous on a first call. It is not. "How did the last tool like this get bought here?" is a neutral, factual question that reliably surfaces a procurement process, a security review, or a person two levels up, any of which changes your timeline by months. Finding out in minute eighteen is free; finding out in week six is expensive.

On talk ratio: the widely repeated figures for how much a seller should talk come in several incompatible versions across secondary write-ups — 43/57, 46/54, 40/60 — and Gong's own discovery-call page does not publish a single number for it. So this guide does not give you one. The directional advice stands without a figure: if you are talking more than the buyer on a discovery call, the call has turned into a presentation.

## Disqualifying

The purpose of the call is to find out whether this is real. Four things should end it early, and ending it early is the single highest-value skill on this page.

**No quantified cost.** They cannot say what the problem costs in money or hours. Not "they don't know offhand" — genuinely cannot, after being asked twice. There is no budget behind an unquantified problem.

**No named owner.** Nobody owns the problem. It annoys three departments and belongs to none. These deals feel promising for months and never close, because consensus purchases need a champion with a reason to spend their credibility.

**No trigger.** Nothing has changed and nothing will. The status quo has survived for years and will survive your product.

**A blocker on your refusal list.** They need something you have deliberately decided not to do. [Scoping a first version](/build/scoping-v1/) argues for writing that list in advance precisely so this moment is a lookup rather than an argument you lose in the room.

How to actually do it: "Honestly, I don't think we're the right fit yet, and here's why." Then say why, specifically. Two things happen. You get your afternoon back. And — consistently, surprisingly — a meaningful fraction of the people you disqualify refer you to someone who is a fit, or come back when the trigger arrives, because you were the person who told them the truth.

The founder failure here is not misjudging fit. It is knowing and continuing anyway, because a bad pipeline feels better than a short one.

## The demo

Short, narrow, and quoted back.

**Open with their words.** "You said the thing that slips is assembling the file. Here's that." You have now framed everything they are about to see as a response rather than a tour.

**Show the outcome before the mechanism.** Start at the finished artifact — the report, the reconciled ledger, the sent rota — then show how little work produced it. Buyers care about the output and are tolerant of ugly paths to it.

**Do not show anything they did not ask about.** This is hard, because you are proud of the other parts. Hold them for the second call.

**Name the limits out loud.** "This doesn't do X, and we've decided not to." Said proactively, this builds more credibility than any feature, and it pre-empts the discovery of the same limitation in week three, when it reads as concealment.

**When asked for something you do not have**, two questions before you answer. "What would you do with it?" — which tells you whether it is a requirement or a preference. And "if we never had it, would that stop you?" — which tells you whether it is a blocker or a wish. Most feature requests on sales calls are wishes stated in the grammar of requirements, and building against the grammar rather than the answer is how roadmaps get wrecked.

## Closing the call

Two minutes, one job: a specific next action, a date, and a name.

Weak: "I'll send some information over." Weak: "let me know what you think." Weak: "shall we reconnect in a few weeks?"

Strong: "Next step as I see it — you send me a sample file, I run it and send back what it produces, and we talk Thursday at 3. Does that work, or is there someone else who should be on it?"

If they will not agree to any dated step, that is a no, and it is better to hear it now. Ask directly: "it sounds like this isn't a priority this quarter — is that fair?" Buyers almost always confirm honestly when given a graceful route, and a confirmed no is worth more than an unconfirmed maybe. [Pricing](/sell/pricing/) covers the separate question of when in this sequence a number should be said out loud.

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. Numbers are invented to be plausible; no company described here exists.*

A founder selling an inventory-forecasting tool to e-commerce brands. First eight calls follow the bad shape: demo at minute four, twenty-five minutes of features, "looks great, let me discuss internally." Eight calls, zero second calls.

She changes the structure. Call nine, with a 40-person homeware brand.

**Minutes 0–2.** "I'd like to spend most of this understanding how you forecast today, then show you only the relevant part. Fine?" Yes.

**Minutes 2–20.** Thirteen questions, and they produce four problems rather than one.

*Problem in their words:* they do not say forecasting. They say **"we're either out of stock on the good stuff or sitting on pallets of the bad stuff."**

*Status quo:* a spreadsheet maintained by the operations manager, rebuilt every quarter, based on last year's same-month sales. Tried a forecasting module in their ERP in 2024 and abandoned it — "it wanted three years of clean data and ours isn't clean."

*Cost of doing nothing:* the founder asks and waits. The buyer works it out aloud: roughly £18,000 of stock written off last year, and two stockouts on their best SKU during Q4 that they estimate at £30,000 of lost sales. She does not supply either number.

*Process and authority:* the operations manager owns it. The founder (their founder) signs anything over £5,000 a year. They bought their last tool after a two-week trial, no procurement process.

*Trigger:* they are launching into a second country in January, which doubles the SKU count, and the operations manager has said plainly that the spreadsheet will not survive it.

**Minutes 20–27.** Demo, three screens only. Opens with: "you said you're either out of the good stuff or sitting on the bad stuff — here's the part that flags both." Shows the output first: a one-page reorder list. Then shows that it took one CSV upload. Then — unprompted — says "this doesn't integrate with your ERP, and we're not building that this year. You'd export and upload weekly, five minutes." Does not show the four other sections.

The buyer's only objection is about the dirty-data problem that killed the last attempt. The founder shows what happens with two months of messy data rather than three years of clean data. That single exchange is the deal.

**Minutes 27–30.** "Next step: you send me last quarter's sales export, I run it and send you the reorder list it produces so you can judge it against what you actually did. Thursday work for a fifteen-minute call on the result? And should your founder be on that one, given the £5,000 line?"

**Outcome.** Export arrives next day. The generated list would have caught one of the two Q4 stockouts. Trial agreed in the second call, paid conversion six weeks later at £3,600 a year — deliberately under the £5,000 approval line, which the founder only knew because she asked the authority question.

**The pattern across the next twelve calls.** Nine run the new structure, three slip back into demo-first when the buyer opens with "so show me what you've got." Of the nine, five reach a second call. Of the three, none do. She also disqualifies four prospects on the call, three for no quantified cost and one for needing ERP integration, and two of those four introduce her to someone else — which is the referral effect that makes early disqualification cheaper than it looks.

**What she got wrong.** For the first month she asked the cost question and then filled the silence herself, suggesting numbers. Buyers agreed with her numbers, she wrote them down as data, and two deals were forecast on figures she had invented and they had politely accepted. Both stalled. Ask, then wait, is a harder discipline than it reads.

## Checklist

Before the call:

- [ ] Known or hypothesised *why now* for this specific company.
- [ ] My refusal list open, so a blocker is a lookup and not an argument.
- [ ] Thirty minutes in the calendar, not sixty.
- [ ] One sentence prepared to frame the call and buy the question time.

During:

- [ ] Framed the structure in the first two minutes and got agreement.
- [ ] Not sharing my screen before minute 18.
- [ ] 11 to 14 questions across 3 to 4 distinct problems.
- [ ] The problem captured in **their exact words**, written down verbatim.
- [ ] The cost of doing nothing stated as a number **by them**, after I asked and waited.
- [ ] Asked how the last comparable tool got bought here.
- [ ] Demoed only against problems they named, outcome first.
- [ ] Named one limitation out loud, unprompted.
- [ ] For every feature request: what would you do with it, and would its absence stop you.

Ending:

- [ ] One concrete next step, with a date and a named owner.
- [ ] Or an explicit no, offered gracefully and confirmed.
- [ ] Their verbatim language added to the sheet within an hour, while it is still accurate.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** Every call ends "looks great, let me discuss internally," then silence.
**Cause:** Demo-first. They have an impression and you have no information, so neither side can advance it.
**Fix:** The structure above. The frame in minute one is what makes it possible.

**Symptom:** You cannot forecast any deal and every one feels equally plausible.
**Cause:** No cost, authority or trigger established, so there is nothing to forecast on.
**Fix:** Call back the three most promising and ask the three questions directly. Expect at least one to evaporate; that is the method working.

**Symptom:** The pipeline keeps growing and nothing closes.
**Cause:** Nothing is being disqualified. Interest is being recorded as pipeline.
**Fix:** Apply the four disqualifiers to every open deal this afternoon. A short honest pipeline is a management tool.

**Symptom:** The roadmap is full of features requested on sales calls that nobody now uses.
**Cause:** Wishes were recorded as requirements, because nobody asked the two follow-up questions.
**Fix:** Ask them retroactively on the next three requests. The attrition rate is startling.

**Symptom:** Deals stall after a good second call, with a vague internal process.
**Cause:** A decision-maker or a security review you learned about too late.
**Fix:** Move the authority question earlier — first call, not third. It is not presumptuous, it is logistics.

**Symptom:** A buyer discovers a limitation in week three and goes cold.
**Cause:** You hoped it would not come up.
**Fix:** State limits in the demo. Volunteering a limitation is the cheapest credibility available to an unknown company.

**Symptom:** Buyers agree with every cost estimate you offer and the deals still die.
**Cause:** You supplied the numbers. Agreement with your figure is politeness, not data.
**Fix:** Ask, then be silent. Count to five if you have to.
