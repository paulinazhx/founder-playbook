## TL;DR

- Write for **one question a buyer actually typed**, not a topic. A topic page competes with everything indexed; a question page competes with the handful of pages answering it badly.
- **Answer it completely, in one page.** Google's published guidance asks whether a reader "learned enough about a topic to help achieve their goal." Withholding the answer to force a conversation fails that test and reads as search-engine-first.
- You already have the raw material and nobody else does: the questions buyers ask on your calls, in their words. That is the cheapest differentiation available and it comes out of work you are already doing in [founder-led sales](/founder-playbook/sell/).
- **Depth beats volume.** Three complete answers outrank thirty restatements, and thin pages cost the credibility of the good pages beside them.
- Judge it on **qualified conversations**, not traffic, and expect **six to twelve months**. It is an asset you build, not a channel you switch on — which makes it the wrong answer for this quarter's revenue and the right one for every quarter after.

## Who this is for

You want buyers to arrive without you chasing them, and you are starting from no audience, no traffic and no domain history. You may also be looking at inbound because [cold outbound](/founder-playbook/sell/outbound/) did the volume arithmetic and the answer came out impossible.

Skip this if you need revenue in the next ninety days. Inbound will not produce it, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. Skip it too if your buyers genuinely do not search — some roles do not, and three conversations with people in the role will tell you.

## The problem

Inbound from zero fails in two opposite directions, and founders usually manage both in sequence.

**The first failure is volume without depth.** The advice says publish consistently, so you publish thirty short posts on topics adjacent to your product. None of them answers a question completely, because each took ninety minutes. Nothing ranks. The diagnosis you reach is that you need more posts, and the real problem is that you have added thirty pages that restate what is already indexed better elsewhere, which does not just fail to help — it establishes your domain as a source of thin material.

**The second failure is building on rented land.** You write well, on a platform that is not yours, where the audience and the distribution belong to someone else. It works, briefly, because the platform's feed is boosting you. Then the feed changes, and the compounding stops, and you own nothing. The asset was never yours.

Underneath both is an expectation problem that causes most of the abandonment. Inbound compounds, which means it looks like failure for a long time and then does not. A founder measuring weekly traffic against a channel that pays out in months will conclude it does not work at roughly month three — which is exactly when a correctly-built page is starting to be indexed and ranked but has not yet accumulated enough signal to rank well.

And there is a third, more interesting problem specific to now: **the cost of publishing has collapsed, so volume has stopped being a differentiator.** Anyone can produce thirty plausible pages this week. What that means is not that writing no longer works; it means the only defensible thing left is the part that cannot be generated from what is already on the internet — the specific, unflattering, first-hand detail of a problem you have actually watched people have. That is the one input you have and your competitors do not.

## What Google says it rewards

Worth reading from the source rather than from the commentary, because the commentary is mostly written by people selling services. Google's [guidance on creating helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) is published and specific, and it is framed as self-assessment questions rather than rules.

The parts that bear directly on what an early-stage company should do:

**On substance.** Does the content offer "original information, reporting, research, or analysis"? Does it provide "insightful analysis or interesting information that is beyond the obvious"? Is it "the sort of page you'd want to bookmark, share with a friend, or recommend"?

**On trust.** Google states that its systems prioritise content demonstrating "experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness" — and, importantly, that "trust is most important. The others contribute to trust, but content doesn't necessarily have to demonstrate all of them." The practical markers it names are "clear sourcing, evidence of the expertise involved, background about the author or the site," and "no easily-verified factual errors."

**On audience.** "Do you have an existing or intended audience for your business or site that would find the content useful if they came directly to you?" And whether a reader leaves "having learned enough about a topic to help achieve their goal."

**On search-engine-first content.** The red flags it lists include "Is the content primarily made to attract visits from search engines?" and "Are you using extensive automation to produce content on many topics?"

**On automation and disclosure.** Google asks: "Is the use of automation, including AI-generation, self-evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways?" Using automation "to produce content for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings" violates its spam policies.

Two things follow that are directly useful. First, the two advantages available to a small unknown company are the ones Google names as decisive: first-hand experience, and traceable sourcing. Both are cheap for you and expensive for a content farm. Second, the position on automation is about **concealment and intent**, not about tooling — which means the right move is to say plainly how the work was produced and who is accountable for it being correct.

## What to write

One question per page, taken from a real conversation.

**The source is your sales calls.** You are already collecting the exact phrasing buyers use — that is the practice in [founder-led sales](/founder-playbook/sell/), and it is the input that makes this channel work. Go through your notes. Every question a buyer asked that took you more than two minutes to answer is a page. Every question you answered badly is a better page, because you had to go and find out.

**Use their nouns.** If buyers say "locum cover" and you say "shift scheduling," the page says locum cover. This is the cheapest ranking improvement available and it costs nothing but attention.

**Answer it completely.** The whole answer, including the part that does not favour you, including when the answer is "don't buy anything for this yet." A page that solves the reader's problem is the only thing that produces the behaviour Google is trying to detect and the only thing that makes a stranger trust you.

**Say what you don't know.** Where there is no credible answer, say so. This is unusual enough to be a differentiator and it is also just correct — the same standard [pricing](/founder-playbook/sell/pricing/) applies when it declines to quote a benchmark whose provenance does not survive a check.

**Structure it so it can be scanned.** What it is, who it is for, the options, what each costs, a checklist. A reader arriving from a search result is deciding in about eight seconds whether this page is the one.

## Where to put it

**On a surface you own.** Your own domain, or a path on it. A documentation subdirectory, a guides section, a library. If the whole thing lives in someone else's platform, the compounding accrues to them.

**Pick one shape and go deep.** A small library of complete guides, with one page per pillar carrying the whole argument and narrower pages beneath it, is a structure that works for a small team because it concentrates effort. Eighteen pages that each answer one question completely is a far better asset than two hundred posts.

**Make the pages findable by each other.** Internal links placed where the question arises, not collected at the bottom. A reader who finishes one page and has a next question should find it in the sentence where the question occurs to them.

**Do the boring technical parts once.** A sitemap, a canonical URL per page, descriptive titles and descriptions, structured data naming the author, and pages that load fast. None of this makes mediocre content rank. All of it removes reasons for good content not to.

## How long, and what to measure

The honest timeline, for a new domain with no history:

| Month | What is realistic |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 | Pages indexed. Essentially no traffic. Nothing to judge. |
| 3–4 | Impressions on long, specific queries. A trickle of visits. Still nothing to judge. |
| 5–8 | First rankings on narrow questions. The first inbound conversations, usually a handful. |
| 9–12 | A channel that produces conversations with some regularity, if the pages are genuinely good. |

**Measure qualified conversations per month.** That is the thing the channel exists to produce. Traffic is available sooner and tells you much less — a page can rank for a question asked by people who will never buy, and the only way you find out is by looking at who gets in touch.

Two secondary numbers worth watching, both from Search Console: which queries you appear for, and which pages get the impressions. The queries are the useful one, because they frequently reveal that buyers describe the problem differently from how you do, which is a finding you can apply to the whole site and to your sales calls. [Startup metrics that change a decision](/founder-playbook/build/metrics-that-matter/) makes the general point — a number with no decision attached is decoration, and traffic is usually decoration.

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. Numbers are invented to be plausible; no company described here exists.*

A two-person company selling compliance documentation software to small medical device manufacturers. Outbound works but has a ceiling — the trigger list regenerates at about forty names a month. They want a channel that does not depend on them sending.

**The wrong start, months 1–3.** They publish eleven posts: "5 Tips for Regulatory Compliance", "Why Documentation Matters", "The Future of MedTech Regulation". Each about 700 words, each written in an afternoon, each a restatement of material already indexed. Traffic after three months: 94 visits, 61 of which are the founders. Zero conversations.

**The reset.** They go through eleven months of sales call notes and extract every question a buyer asked that took real effort to answer. Twenty-three questions. The top four by how often they came up:

1. "Do we need a notified body for a Class IIa device if we're only selling in the UK?"
2. "What actually has to be in the technical file, section by section?"
3. "How long does a submission take if the documentation is already in order?"
4. "Can we use a consultant's template, or does it have to be ours?"

None of these are marketing topics. All of them are the actual question, in the buyer's words, including the acronyms.

**The rebuild, months 4–6.** They delete all eleven old posts — a decision that takes an argument, and is correct, because thin pages cost the credibility of the good ones. They write four pages, one per question, each 2,000 to 3,000 words, each answering the question completely including the parts where the answer is "you don't need us for this."

Every factual claim links the primary source: the relevant UK MHRA guidance, the regulation text itself, not a law firm's summary of it. Each page names the author, says what they do, and says plainly where the authors' knowledge comes from and where it stops.

**Months 7–9.** Impressions start on the long queries. The technical-file page begins ranking for variations nobody would bid on. First inbound conversation arrives in month 8 — someone who read the technical file page, worked through most of it themselves, and got in touch because section four was the part they could not do alone.

**Month 12.** Four pages, plus two more added since. Roughly 2,400 visits a month and **nine inbound conversations**, of which four become sales calls and two close. Compared against outbound in the same month: 38 sends, five conversations, two sales calls, one close.

The finding that surprised them: Search Console showed they were appearing for "technical file" queries far more than "compliance software" queries. Their buyers were not searching for a product; they were searching for the task. They changed the site's whole top-level framing to match, and the outbound opener too.

**What they got wrong.** They kept the eleven thin posts live for two months after deciding to delete them, because deleting published work felt wasteful. During that window a prospect mentioned having read one and described it, accurately, as "quite generic" — which was the push they needed. The sunk cost was ninety minutes a post; the cost of keeping them was the impression they made on exactly the readers they were trying to reach.

## Checklist

Before writing anything:

- [ ] A list of questions taken from real sales calls, in the buyer's words.
- [ ] Each candidate page aimed at **one** question, not a topic.
- [ ] Checked that buyers in this role actually search — three people in the role asked.
- [ ] An honest timeline agreed internally: six to twelve months, measured in conversations.

Per page:

- [ ] Answers the question completely, including the parts that do not favour us.
- [ ] Says plainly where the answer is uncertain or where no credible answer exists.
- [ ] Every number or claim links a primary source, with its sample named.
- [ ] The buyer's own nouns used throughout.
- [ ] Author named, with background, and accountability for accuracy stated.
- [ ] Scannable: what it is, who it is for, the options, the costs, a checklist.
- [ ] Internal links placed where the next question occurs, not collected at the end.

Technical, once:

- [ ] Own domain or own path. Not someone else's platform.
- [ ] Sitemap submitted in Search Console.
- [ ] One canonical URL per page; descriptive title and description.
- [ ] Structured data naming the author and the publisher.
- [ ] Fast pages, no unnecessary client-side JavaScript.
- [ ] If automation was used in production, disclosed plainly.

Judging it:

- [ ] Qualified conversations per month is the metric. Traffic is secondary.
- [ ] Search Console queries reviewed monthly for the words buyers actually use.
- [ ] Thin pages deleted rather than left up.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** Thirty posts, no rankings, no conversations.
**Cause:** Volume without depth. Each page restates what is already indexed.
**Fix:** Delete the thin ones. Replace with a handful of complete answers to questions buyers actually asked.

**Symptom:** Traffic is growing and nobody gets in touch.
**Cause:** Ranking for questions asked by people who will not buy, or pages that inform without connecting to anything.
**Fix:** Check Search Console queries against your buyer. Rewrite toward the questions a buyer asks, which are usually narrower and lower-volume.

**Symptom:** It worked and then stopped.
**Cause:** Usually rented land — a platform's distribution changed.
**Fix:** Move the asset to a surface you own. Accept a slower, durable start.

**Symptom:** You abandoned it at month three.
**Cause:** Weekly measurement of a channel that pays out in months.
**Fix:** Commit to a window, measure conversations not traffic, and decide at the end of the window rather than inside it.

**Symptom:** A prospect described your content as generic.
**Cause:** It contains nothing that could only have come from you.
**Fix:** Put the first-hand specifics in — the numbers, the awkward exceptions, the thing you got wrong. That is the one input competitors cannot copy.

**Symptom:** Rankings slipped after publishing a lot quickly.
**Cause:** Thin pages dragging on the domain, which is one of the behaviours Google's guidance names explicitly.
**Fix:** Prune. Fewer, better pages, with sourcing and authorship clear.
