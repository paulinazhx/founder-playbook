## TL;DR

- The first ten deals are **research with a payment attached**. Revenue is the evidence, not the objective. This is why they cannot be delegated: the information arrives mid-call, and only a founder can act on it while the call is still happening.
- Work **ten names, not a funnel**. Ten is not a statistical quantity. Conversion rates across ten rows are noise, and treating them as data is how founders conclude the wrong thing confidently.
- Run calls to find the **disqualifier**. Going in to find why this is not a fit shortens bad deals, surfaces the real pattern in who buys, and — counter-intuitively — closes more, because buyers trust someone who will tell them no.
- Record the buyer's **exact words** for the problem and for what they do today. That language becomes your site, your outbound and your pitch. Your paraphrase of it is worth much less and cannot be reconstructed later.
- Hand off when the motion is **describable without you**: target, opener, three objections, three answers, written down, and someone else wins a deal you were not in. A revenue number is not the gate.

## Who this is for

You have something to sell, or nearly, and no salespeople. Maybe you have never sold anything and find the idea faintly humiliating. Maybe you have sold before, at a company with a brand, a deck and a price list, and are discovering how much of that was load-bearing.

Skip this if you have an inbound motion that is already working at volume — your problem is a funnel, and funnels have their own literature. Skip it too if you are pre-product and your question is what to build; that is [scoping a first version](/founder-playbook/build/scoping-v1/), and the honest sequence is that you should be having these conversations *before* you build, not after.

## The problem

Founders avoid selling, and the stated reasons are never the real one.

The stated reasons are that the product is not ready, that it would be dishonest to sell something incomplete, that there is engineering to finish first. The real reason is that selling is the only activity in a startup with **immediate, personal, unambiguous rejection**. Building does not reject you. Fundraising rejects you slowly and politely. A prospect who goes quiet after a demo rejects you specifically, about the thing you made, and you cannot interpret it as anything else. So founders build, because building always has another task in it.

The cost of waiting is not mainly lost revenue. It is that you keep building without the information that would tell you what to build. [CB Insights' March 2026 analysis of 431 failed VC-backed companies](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/) found poor product-market fit cited in **43%** of cases, behind running out of capital at 70% — which the report describes as "almost always the final cause of death, not the root problem." The root problem is frequently that nobody tested the premise against a person with a budget until the capital was mostly spent.

There is a second, subtler problem, and it is the one that makes this guide necessary rather than obvious. Most sales advice is written for a situation you are not in. It assumes a known buyer, an established value proposition, a price, a comparison set, and a rep whose job is to convert demand that already exists. You have none of those. Your job is not conversion. It is **finding out whether the demand exists at all, and in whom** — and the techniques for that are different, sometimes opposite.

Paul Graham's [*Do Things That Don't Scale*](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html) makes the general version of the argument: the things that work at the beginning are deliberately unscalable, and founders resist them because they feel like cheating rather than like building a company. Recruiting users one at a time is the canonical example. It feels like it does not count. It is the only thing that does, at ten customers.

## What the work actually is

Founder-led selling is five distinct jobs that get collapsed into the word "sales." They fail for different reasons and most founders are bad at a different one than they think.

| Job | What it is | Where founders go wrong | Where it is covered |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Find** | Deciding which specific companies and people have the problem. | Defining the target as a category ("mid-market fintech") rather than as a list of names with a reason beside each. | Below, and [outbound](/founder-playbook/sell/outbound/) for sourcing at volume. |
| **Reach** | Getting a reply from a person who does not know you. | Treating a low reply rate as a message problem when it is almost always a list problem. | [Cold outbound](/founder-playbook/sell/outbound/) |
| **Qualify** | Finding out fast whether this is real, and disqualifying if not. | Trying to persuade instead of diagnose, so bad deals last months. | [Discovery and demos](/founder-playbook/sell/discovery-and-demos/) |
| **Run** | The call itself: understanding the problem, showing the thing against it. | Demoing features in the order they were built rather than against a stated problem. | [Discovery and demos](/founder-playbook/sell/discovery-and-demos/) |
| **Close** | A price, a decision, a date, a signature. | Never asking. Ending calls on "I'll send something over" and treating silence as deliberation. | Below, and [pricing](/founder-playbook/sell/pricing/) |

The one most founders are worst at is **close**, and the reason is specific: asking for a decision converts a pleasant ambiguous relationship into a definite answer that might be no. Ambiguity feels like pipeline. It is not. A prospect who has not said no is not therefore saying yes; they are usually saying nothing, and the cost of that is a quarter of your calendar spent on deals that were never going to happen.

## Why it has to be you

Three reasons, and only the third is the deep one.

**You can change the promise mid-call.** A prospect says "this is interesting but what we actually need is X." A salesperson writes down a feature request. A founder can say "we could do X by Thursday if you'd commit to a trial" — and either close a deal or discover, from how hard they resist committing, that X was politeness rather than a requirement. That move is unavailable to anyone else in the company, and at ten customers it is the single highest-value move in the building.

**You can make exceptions.** Price, terms, scope, a promise to do something by hand. All of these require authority nobody else has yet, and all of them are how early deals get done.

**Only you can hear the thing that is not about the product.** The most valuable output of an early sales call is rarely "they want feature Y." It is the shape of the problem in their language: who else is involved, what they tried before, what they currently pay for in money or labour, what happens if they do nothing. That information is what your website should say, what your outbound should open with, and what your roadmap should be ordered by. Delegate the calls and you get a summary. A summary is a lossy compression of exactly the part that mattered, and the loss is not recoverable.

## The first ten, concretely

**Make a list of ten names.** Not a segment — ten companies, and inside each, the person who owns the problem. Beside each name, one sentence on why you believe they have it. If you cannot write that sentence, they are on the list because they were easy to find, which is the most common defect in a first target list.

Where the names come from, in descending order of usefulness: people you already know who have the problem; people one introduction away; companies you can see have the problem from public evidence — a job posting, a conference talk, a product gap, a complaint on a forum; then cold.

**Promise something you can deliver by hand.** If the product does half of it, do the other half yourself. Manually, over email, at 11pm if necessary. This is not cheating, it is the correct approach, and the argument is in [scoping a first version](/founder-playbook/build/scoping-v1/): doing it by hand is how you learn what the automated version must handle. It also means the thing you are testing is "will someone pay for this outcome" rather than "is my software finished," and only the first question matters yet.

**Charge money.** Even a small amount. The transition from free to paid is where you find out whether the problem is real, and it is a different conversation entirely — free pilots generate enthusiasm from people with no stake. Discount freely; the price is not the information yet. What matters is that a budget holder agreed to move money. [Pricing](/founder-playbook/sell/pricing/) covers what the number should actually be.

**Track it in a spreadsheet.** One row per company. Who, how you reached them, the problem **in their words**, where it stalled, what they do instead today, and the date of the next concrete step. Six columns. A CRM here is premature: its fields encode a stage model you have not earned, and you will start managing the model instead of the deals. The tool-stack argument for this is in [buy, build, or skip](/founder-playbook/build/).

**Do not compute conversion rates.** With ten rows, three closes and seven losses is indistinguishable from five and five. Founders who compute percentages at this volume reliably conclude something confident and wrong. Look for *patterns in kind* instead: did the three who bought have anything in common that the seven did not?

## Reading the results

After ten real attempts you are looking for one thing: a repeated reason. Not a repeated outcome.

**Pattern in who bought.** If the closes share a characteristic the losses do not — size, role, a particular trigger event, a specific thing they had already tried — that is your segment, and it is almost always narrower than what you wrote down at the start. This is the single most valuable output of the exercise.

**Pattern in why they lost.** Sort the losses into three piles, because they mean completely different things. *Wrong person* means your targeting is off and is cheap to fix. *Right person, wrong time* means a trigger you should learn to detect. *Right person, right time, not worth it* is the one that matters: the premise is weak for that segment and no sales technique will repair it.

**Pattern in the language.** Collect the exact phrasings. When three buyers describe the problem with the same noun that is not the noun you use, change your noun. This is the cheapest conversion improvement available to an early company and it costs nothing but attention.

## When to hand it off

The wrong trigger is a revenue number. The right trigger is **describability**.

You are ready when you can write, on one page: who to contact, with the reason they have the problem; what to say first; the three objections that come up, in order of frequency; the answer to each; what the price is and when to say it; and what "qualified" means specifically enough that someone else could apply it.

Then test it: hand that page to one person and have them win a deal you were not in. If they cannot, the page is wrong — which is useful, because it means you had not finished discovery and were about to pay someone to do it for you. [First hires](/founder-playbook/run/first-hires/) covers the role itself, including why a first sales hire made too early is the most common expensive hiring mistake founders make.

The thing to be honest about: founders usually hand off because selling is uncomfortable, and dress it up as a scaling decision. The tell is hiring a salesperson while being unable to write the page.

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. Numbers are invented to be plausible; no company described here exists.*

Two founders, a product that automates shift scheduling for independent pharmacies. Neither has sold before. They decide to work ten names before writing any more code.

**The list.** Their first draft is "independent pharmacies in the UK" — a category, not a list. Reworked into ten named pharmacies with a reason each: four from a pharmacy group one founder's family uses, three found by searching for pharmacies advertising for a locum pharmacist that week (a trigger event — understaffing), three from a pharmacy owners' forum where scheduling complaints appear.

**Reach.** 10 names, 7 replies, 5 calls booked. That reply rate — 70% — is nothing like cold outbound and that is the point: eight of the ten were warm or trigger-based. Worth comparing to the arithmetic of cold sending, where Gong's analysis of 28 million cold emails puts the average rep at **344 emails per booked meeting**. Those two numbers are not in competition; they describe different activities, and the first ten should come from the first kind.

**The calls.** Five calls. The founders go in with a demo and get through it in the first two. Both end in "send me something." Neither replies. After the second they change approach: no demo for the first twenty minutes, just the current process, who does it, how long it takes, what goes wrong.

What they hear on call three changes the company. The owner does not describe scheduling as the problem. She describes **locum cover** — when someone calls in sick, she spends an hour phoning agencies, and the agency rate is three times her staff rate. The scheduling is annoying. The locum scramble costs her money, and she can say how much: roughly £400 a month in premium rates she believes is avoidable.

Calls four and five, they ask about locum cover directly. Both volunteer the same thing unprompted, one of them in almost identical words.

**The deals.** They sell to three of the five, at £49 a month, which they picked as "low enough to not need a procurement conversation." All three deals are for a product that does not exist: the founders promise a weekly schedule and a locum-cover rota, and for the first six weeks they produce the rota **by hand** from a spreadsheet, on Sunday evenings, for three customers. Two hours a week, total.

**What the spreadsheet showed.** Of the two losses: one was a single-pharmacist shop with no staff to schedule — wrong person, cheap learning. The other was a nine-branch group whose head office mandates a rota system — right problem, wrong buyer, because the decision sits two levels up. Of the three closes: all three were independents with four to twelve staff and no head office. That is the segment, and it is far narrower than "independent pharmacies."

**The language change.** They stop saying "scheduling" entirely. The site, the outbound and the pitch all open on locum cover. Reply rate on the next twenty cold emails goes from 2 in 20 to 7 in 20. Same product, same price, same founders. The word changed.

**Hand-off, eleven months later.** 38 customers, roughly £2,100 of monthly recurring revenue, and the founders can write the page: target is independents with 4 to 12 staff, no head office; open with the locum question; three objections are "we use WhatsApp" (answer: the cost is the agency premium, not the admin), "my staff won't use an app" (answer: they don't have to, the rota goes out by text), and "can you integrate with our till system" (answer: no, and here is why that does not matter yet — straight off the refusal list). They hire one salesperson, who closes a deal in week three that neither founder touched.

What they got wrong: they kept producing rotas by hand until customer fourteen, four months longer than they should have, because it was working and each Sunday was only two hours. By fourteen customers it was nine hours of a founder's weekend and had quietly become the reason no new feature shipped that quarter. Doing things by hand is correct early and has an expiry date that nobody announces.

## Checklist

- [ ] Ten named companies and ten named people, each with a one-sentence reason they have the problem.
- [ ] At least six of the ten warm, introduced, or trigger-based rather than cold.
- [ ] A promise I can deliver by hand this week if the software cannot.
- [ ] A price. Money changes hands, however small the amount.
- [ ] Six-column spreadsheet: company, person, how reached, problem **in their words**, where it stalled, what they do today.
- [ ] Every call run to find the disqualifier, not the yes.
- [ ] Every call ends with a concrete next step and a date, or an explicit no. "I'll send something over" is not a next step.
- [ ] No conversion percentages computed. Patterns in kind only.
- [ ] Losses sorted into wrong person / wrong time / not worth it.
- [ ] The buyer's nouns adopted wherever they differ from mine.
- [ ] The one-page motion written, and tested by someone else winning a deal I was not in, before any sales hire.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** Lots of friendly calls, no decisions.
**Cause:** Never asking. The calls are pleasant because nobody has been asked to commit to anything.
**Fix:** End every call by asking for a specific next step with a date. Accept a no gladly; it is a cheaper outcome than a maybe that lasts a quarter.

**Symptom:** Everyone says the problem is real and nobody buys.
**Cause:** Real but not expensive. "Yes that's annoying" is not the same as a budget, and the gap between them is where early companies die quietly.
**Fix:** Ask what it costs them today, in money or hours, and what they currently pay to make it less bad. If the answer is nothing, the pain is not funded and you need a different segment or a different problem.

**Symptom:** Each deal requires a different promise.
**Cause:** Either the segment is too broad, or you are selling consulting with software attached.
**Fix:** Look at what the closes have in common and narrow to that. Three customers in one segment is worth more than ten in six.

**Symptom:** A salesperson was hired and is not closing.
**Cause:** Usually the motion was not describable, so they are doing discovery on commission, which nobody is good at.
**Fix:** Write the page. If you cannot, go back to selling yourself for another quarter. This is a hard conversation and much cheaper than the alternative.

**Symptom:** The pipeline is full and nothing moves.
**Cause:** No disqualification. Everything that ever expressed interest is still in the sheet.
**Fix:** Close out every deal with no next step and no date. A short honest pipeline is a management tool; a long optimistic one is a comfort object.

**Symptom:** You are doing more manual delivery each week and shipping less.
**Cause:** The unscalable thing has outlived its usefulness — correct early, expensive once it is a quarter of your week.
**Fix:** Pick the single most repeated manual step and automate only that. Re-check monthly, because the expiry date is never announced.
