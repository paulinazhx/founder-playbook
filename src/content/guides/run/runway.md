## TL;DR

- Compute runway from **bank statements**, not a forecast: cash divided by average net outflow over the last three completed months. Then note the direction, because rising burn means less runway than the division says.
- Cut in order of **reversibility** — what can stop this month, what takes a quarter, what cannot be undone. That ordering buys months without spending an option you still hold.
- **Raise prices before cutting growth.** It is faster, cheaper and less damaging than reducing the activity that brings customers in, and founders consider it last because it feels like it risks revenue. See [pricing](/founder-playbook/sell/pricing/).
- Name **what the extended runway has to buy** — a specific thing that must be true before the money gets low. Cutting without a destination buys time that then gets spent the same way.
- **Cut once, deeply enough, and say the number out loud.** Two shallow rounds cost more trust than one adequate one, because after the first nobody believes the second is the last.

## Who this is for

You have less money than you would like and you are deciding what to do about it. Possibly you have realised you do not know your runway to the week, which is itself the finding — it is one of the four duties in [the CEO job](/founder-playbook/run/) and the one most often unattended.

Skip this if you are default alive and growing; your question is how fast to spend, which is a different and happier problem. Skip it too if the answer is clearly "raise now" because a round is already in motion — go to [raising a first round](/founder-playbook/run/fundraising/).

## The problem

Runway is mismanaged in a specific sequence, and the first step is not a spending decision. It is an arithmetic one.

**The number is usually wrong, and wrong in the optimistic direction.** Founders compute runway from a model. The model contains revenue growth that has not happened yet and costs that are stated as plans rather than as transactions. It also usually omits things that are real: annual renewals landing in one month, payroll taxes, the contractor invoice that arrives quarterly, the payment-processing fees that scale with revenue. A model-derived runway is routinely two to four months longer than the bank will support, and the error is discovered at the worst time.

**The cuts happen too late and too shallow.** Cutting is an admission, so it is deferred until the number is frightening. Then it happens under pressure, which produces the worst version: a cut large enough to damage morale and small enough to need repeating. The second round is far more expensive than the first, because after one round nobody believes the next one is the last, and the best people start interviewing.

**The cuts are made in the wrong order.** Under pressure, founders reach for the biggest line, which is payroll, which is also the least reversible and the most damaging to the thing the runway is supposed to buy. Meanwhile the reversible stuff — subscriptions, duplicated tools, discretionary spend — sits untouched because individually each item is small.

And the thing that makes all of this consequential rather than merely untidy: **remaining runway is an input to your fundraising terms.** [CB Insights' March 2026 analysis of 431 failed VC-backed companies](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/) found **70%** ran out of capital, while noting that capital exhaustion "is almost always the final cause of death, not the root problem." The root problem is frequently that nobody acted while the options were still cheap.

## Get the number right

Four facts, from the bank, every week. It takes thirty minutes.

**Cash.** What is actually in the accounts today, minus anything already committed and not yet paid.

**Net burn.** Average monthly net outflow over the last **three completed months**, taken from statements. Not the model. Not last month alone, which is distorted by whatever annual thing landed in it.

**Months.** Cash divided by net burn.

**Direction.** Is burn rising or falling, and is revenue rising or falling? The division assumes both are flat, which they never are. A company burning £40,000 a month with burn rising £3,000 a month has materially less runway than the quotient suggests, and one with growing revenue has more.

Two adjustments that catch people out. **Annual payments** — insurance, audits, software renewals — should be spread across the months they cover when you compute the average, and then flagged separately so you know which month they land in. And **payroll taxes and employer contributions** are part of the cost of a person, not a footnote; a salary number without them understates by a meaningful margin in most jurisdictions.

Then write the number down somewhere you see it weekly, and put it in the subject line of your [investor update](/founder-playbook/run/investor-updates/). A number you state publicly every month is a number you cannot avoid looking at.

## Cut in order of reversibility

The ordering is the whole technique. Work down the list, and stop when you have bought the months you need.

| Order | What | Typical saving | Reversibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | Software, subscriptions, duplicated tools, unused seats | 10–30% of non-payroll | Immediate. Cancel today, re-subscribe tomorrow. |
| **2** | Discretionary — travel, events, recruiting fees, contractors on non-essential work | Varies, often substantial | Immediate, and nothing structural is lost. |
| **3** | Price increases on new customers | Revenue-side, no cost cut at all | Reversible, and usually should not be reversed. |
| **4** | Renegotiate what you keep — monthly instead of annual, lower tiers, payment terms | 5–15% of remaining | A conversation, not a commitment. |
| **5** | Reduce founder pay | Material at small headcount | Reversible, and a strong signal to a team being asked to accept constraints. |
| **6** | Slow or stop hiring | Large, and it is a decision not to grow | Reversible in principle; the candidate is gone. |
| **7** | Reduce headcount | Largest | **Not reversible.** The person, their knowledge, and how the team reads you. |

Three observations that change outcomes.

**Rows 1 and 2 are almost always larger than founders expect**, and they are free. The audit in [the founder tool stack](/founder-playbook/build/) is the mechanism: one list of every subscription, sorted by category, with duplicates and unused seats visible. A company at ten people routinely finds 20% to 30% of non-payroll spend is duplicated, unused or forgotten.

**Row 3 is the one that gets skipped, and it is often the biggest single lever.** A price increase on new customers only is fast, costs nothing, risks none of your existing revenue, and is reversible. Founders put it last because raising prices feels like it threatens revenue, when the thing it actually threatens is the close rate — and [pricing](/founder-playbook/sell/pricing/) sets out why a close rate falling while revenue per deal rises is a win rather than a warning.

**Row 7 is a different kind of decision and should be treated as one.** If you reach it, cut once and deeply enough. A cut that needs repeating in four months costs more than a larger cut now, because the second one tells everyone remaining that nobody knows how deep the hole is. [First hires](/founder-playbook/run/first-hires/) covers the humane mechanics; the relevant part here is that severance beyond the minimum, where affordable, is what your reputation among future candidates is made of.

## What the runway has to buy

Cutting without a destination is just delay.

Before you cut, write one sentence: **what must be true by the time this money runs low?** It should be a specific, checkable thing. Not "more traction." Something like: "a sales motion describable on one page, validated by a non-founder closing a deal" — the test in [founder-led sales](/founder-playbook/sell/). Or "one cohort whose retention has flattened" — the read in [startup metrics](/founder-playbook/build/metrics-that-matter/). Or "a profitable month."

Then check the arithmetic: does the runway you now have plausibly cover that, plus the three to six months a raise takes? If not, you need either a deeper cut or a smaller destination, and knowing which is a decision you can only make with both numbers in front of you.

**Default alive** is the strongest version of the destination: on current revenue, growth and costs, the company reaches profitability before the cash runs out. It is worth computing even when the answer is no, because it tells you how far away it is. Its real value is not survival — it is that it converts fundraising from a necessity into an option, which changes both the terms you are offered and your ability to walk away from them.

## Telling people

**The team.** Say the number. Founders hide runway out of a belief that it will frighten people, and the result is that everyone knows something is wrong and nobody knows what, which is worse. Give the figure, what you are doing, and what has to be true. People make better decisions with real information, including the decision to stay.

**Investors.** In the [monthly update](/founder-playbook/run/investor-updates/), in the subject line, every month, good or bad. Existing investors who learn about a cash problem late are both unable to help and newly unsure what else they have not been told.

**Customers.** Nothing, unless a cut affects them. If it does — a service level, a feature, a person they worked with — tell them before they notice.

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. Numbers are invented to be plausible; no company described here exists.*

Nine people, £47,000 of monthly recurring revenue, £312,000 in the bank. The founder's model says eleven months of runway.

**The real number.** Three months of statements: average net outflow £38,400, not the £28,000 the model assumed. The gap is annual software renewals landing in two of the three months, employer pension and national insurance contributions the model treated as a footnote, and payment-processing fees that had grown with revenue.

£312,000 ÷ £38,400 = **8.1 months**, not 11. And burn has risen about £1,900 a month over the period, so the honest figure is closer to seven and a half.

The destination they need: a describable sales motion, which they estimate at four to five months away. Plus three to six months to raise. They need twelve months and have seven and a half.

**The cuts, in order.**

*Row 1, software.* The list has 41 subscriptions at £5,900 a month. Two project trackers, three document stores, nineteen unused seats across four tools, a £400-a-month analytics product nobody had opened in five months, and an annual contract auto-renewing in six weeks at nearly double. Cancelled: 16 subscriptions, £2,180 a month. **Zero** growth impact, two days of work.

*Row 2, discretionary.* Two conference sponsorships at £4,000 each, a recruiter retainer for a role now paused, contractor design work on a redesign nobody was waiting for. £1,900 a month equivalent.

*Row 3, prices.* They raise new-customer pricing from £390 to £560 a month. Close rate falls from about 40% to about 31%; revenue per deal rises 44%. Over the next four months this adds roughly £2,400 of monthly recurring revenue against what the old price would have produced. Existing customers untouched and explicitly grandfathered with a stated end date.

*Row 4, renegotiation.* Moved three annual contracts to monthly, dropped two tools a tier. £620 a month, and it cost four emails.

*Row 5, founder pay.* Both founders down 30%, which is £2,800 a month combined. They do this before row 6 and announce it when they announce the hiring freeze, which is the reason row 6 lands the way it does.

*Row 6, hiring.* Two open roles paused. No immediate cash saving; it avoids about £9,000 a month of future burn.

**The result.** Net burn from £38,400 to £30,900, and revenue growing faster than before because of the price change. Runway from 7.5 months to about 12.5. **Row 7 was not reached.**

**The announcement.** All-hands, the real number, the cuts, the founder pay reduction first, the destination, and what they would need to see to resume hiring. One person asks directly whether there will be redundancies; the founder says not on the current plan, and names what would change that. Nobody leaves. Two people independently cancel tools the founder had missed.

**What they got wrong.** They did the price increase six weeks after the cost cuts rather than alongside them, because it felt like the risky move and they wanted to see the cuts land first. Those six weeks at the old price cost roughly £1,400 in deals closed at £390 that would have closed at £560 — the arithmetic in [pricing](/founder-playbook/sell/pricing/) says the close rate would have held up well enough, and it did when they finally moved. The sequencing instinct was backwards: the reversible revenue lever should come first, not last.

## Checklist

The number:

- [ ] Cash today, from the bank, minus committed-and-unpaid.
- [ ] Net burn as the average of the **last three completed months**, from statements.
- [ ] Annual payments spread across the months they cover, and flagged for when they land.
- [ ] Employer taxes and contributions counted as part of payroll, not a footnote.
- [ ] Direction of travel noted for both burn and revenue.
- [ ] Refreshed weekly, by me, in thirty minutes.

Before cutting:

- [ ] One sentence naming what this runway must buy — specific and checkable.
- [ ] Arithmetic checked: destination plus three to six months for a raise, against the months you have.
- [ ] Default-alive position computed, even if the answer is no.

The cuts, in order:

- [ ] One list of every subscription, with duplicates and unused seats visible.
- [ ] Discretionary spend cut.
- [ ] **Price increase on new customers considered before any growth spending is touched.**
- [ ] Contracts renegotiated: monthly billing, lower tiers, terms.
- [ ] Founder pay reduced before anyone else is asked to accept anything.
- [ ] Hiring paused.
- [ ] Headcount only if required — and then once, deeply enough, with severance beyond the minimum where affordable.

Afterwards:

- [ ] The runway number said out loud to the team, with the destination.
- [ ] The number in the subject line of every investor update.
- [ ] Customers told before they notice anything that affects them.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** Your runway number turned out to be months shorter than you thought.
**Cause:** Computed from a model containing unearned revenue and planned costs.
**Fix:** Three months of statements, every week. The model is for planning; the statements are for deciding.

**Symptom:** You cut and had to cut again four months later.
**Cause:** The first cut was sized to feel survivable rather than to buy the months needed.
**Fix:** Size the cut to the destination plus the raise. One adequate round costs far less trust than two shallow ones.

**Symptom:** You cut headcount and the reversible spending is still running.
**Cause:** Reaching for the biggest line under pressure instead of working the order.
**Fix:** The subscription audit, now. It is free and it is frequently a month or two of runway.

**Symptom:** The team knows something is wrong and nobody knows what.
**Cause:** The number was withheld to protect morale, which does the opposite.
**Fix:** Say it, with what you are doing and what has to be true.

**Symptom:** You are raising with four months left and the terms are poor.
**Cause:** Runway is an input to the terms, and yours is visible.
**Fix:** If you can still cut to extend, do that and restart from strength. [Raising a first round](/founder-playbook/run/fundraising/) covers why the timing dominates the technique.

**Symptom:** You extended runway and nothing changed about the company's position.
**Cause:** No destination. The time bought was spent the same way as the time before it.
**Fix:** Write the sentence. Time without a destination is the most expensive thing you can buy.
