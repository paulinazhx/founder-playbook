## TL;DR

- **Send on the same date every month, including the bad months.** Reliability is the product. A skipped month is read as the bad news it usually is, and the reader's guess is worse than the truth.
- **Lead with the worst thing.** First three lines, plus what you are doing about it. An investor who hears it from you keeps trusting the rest; one who finds out later re-reads every earlier update as marketing.
- **Runway in months, at the top, every time.** Cash, burn, months left. Every reader is computing it anyway; supplying it is what makes the rest legible.
- **Define metrics once and never move a definition silently.** A number whose meaning drifts reads as spin, and costs the same as spin.
- **One or two asks, specific enough to act on in five seconds.** "An introduction to anyone who has run support at a company selling to pharmacies" gets an email. "Help with hiring" gets sympathy.
- Under **500 words**. Longer is read less carefully, not more.

## Who this is for

You have investors, or people who might become investors, and you are either not sending updates or sending them irregularly and finding them unpleasant to write. Possibly you last sent one four months ago, after a good month.

Skip this if you have a board and a formal reporting pack — you have a different and heavier obligation, and this is the lighter instrument that sits underneath it. The guide still applies to the people who are not on your board.

## The problem

Investor updates fail in a pattern so consistent it is almost a law: **they stop exactly when they start mattering.**

Month one is easy, because there is news. Month four is easy, because the number went up. Month seven, the number went sideways, a key hire fell through and churn was ugly. Writing that update means writing down that the quarter went badly, to the people who gave you money, and the alternative — not sending it — costs nothing visible. So you skip it, intending to send a good one next month. Next month is also not good. By month ten you have not sent an update in a quarter, and now sending one requires explaining the silence as well as the news, which raises the cost again.

Meanwhile, here is what the silence actually does. Your investors have a portfolio. They notice who goes quiet. **Silence is never read as neutral** — it is read as bad, and the reader's imagined version is almost always worse than your actual situation, because their prior is formed by the companies that went quiet and then died.

So the cost of a skipped bad month is not zero. It is the same as sending bad news, except you also forfeit the help, the credit for candour, and the record.

There is a second cost, which only shows up during a raise and which is the reason this is a [fundraising](/run/fundraising/) activity disguised as an admin one. Twelve months of honest monthly updates is the single strongest reference an investor can read about you, and it is the only one that **cannot be manufactured on demand**. During the raise you will wish you had it, and there will be no way to produce it. That asymmetry is the whole argument for starting today, regardless of the month you are in.

## What the update is for

Three jobs. Writing with all three in mind is what separates a useful update from a status report.

**Keep trust.** Primarily by being predictable. A reader who has received eleven consecutive updates, two of which were bad and said so, knows something about you that no meeting conveys. This is the main job and it is achieved mostly by *sending*, not by content.

**Get help.** Investors have networks and pattern memory across dozens of companies. They cannot deploy either against a problem they do not know about or an ask they cannot act on. Most updates fail this job not by omitting the ask but by making it unactionable.

**Create a record.** For you. Twelve updates is a contemporaneous account of what you believed and when, and it is the only defence against the retrospective story-telling every founder does. Reading your own updates from a year ago is reliably uncomfortable and reliably instructive.

## The structure

Five sections, under 500 words, same order every month. The sameness is a feature: a reader who knows where the runway number is can read your update in forty seconds, and a reader who can read it in forty seconds reads it.

**1. The header line.** Month, cash in bank, monthly net burn, runway in months. Four facts, one line.

**2. Metrics.** Two or three, with their definitions stated in the same words every month, and last month's figure beside this month's. Not ten metrics — two or three that you actually run the company by. If you change a definition, say so explicitly and restate the prior months on the new basis.

**3. The most important thing that happened.** One short paragraph. Not a list of everything; the one thing that changed what you believe.

**4. What went wrong.** One short paragraph, and it goes in every update, including good months — a month with nothing in this section reads as a month where nobody looked. What happened, what it cost, what you are doing about it, and by when.

**5. Asks.** One or two, numbered, each specific enough to act on immediately.

If a month genuinely has nothing in section 3 or 4, say that in a sentence and send it anyway. "Quiet month, numbers flat, nothing broke" is a perfectly good update and takes ten minutes.

## Bad news

The rule is to lead with it, and the reason is asymmetry rather than virtue.

Consider the two paths. You disclose: the investor learns the company has a problem, learns you identified it, and may be able to help. Your credibility is unchanged or improved. Or they find out later — from a customer, a candidate, another investor, or a number in a subsequent update that cannot be explained any other way. Now they learn the company has a problem *and* that your updates omit problems, which retroactively devalues every previous update. The second outcome is many times more expensive than the first, and the expense is permanent.

Mechanically:

**Put it in the first three lines** when it is the month's main event. Not buried in section 4 after two paragraphs of progress.

**Say what it cost**, in a number where one exists. "Churn was 4 of 31 accounts, about £1,900 of monthly recurring revenue" rather than "we saw some churn."

**Say what you are doing and by when.** A problem with an owner and a date reads as managed. The same problem with neither reads as drift.

**Do not pre-emptively explain it away.** Macro conditions, seasonality and a competitor's price change may all be true and they are not what the reader wants in the first paragraph. Cause after fact, never instead of it.

The hardest version is the one where the bad news is you. A hire you made is not working and it was your call; a direction you set was wrong. Saying so plainly is the highest-trust move available to a founder, and it is also just the [CEO job](/run/) — being the last decision means owning the ones that were wrong.

## Asks that work

The difference between an ask that gets answered and one that does not is whether the reader can tell, in about five seconds, whether they are the right person.

| Does not work | Works |
| --- | --- |
| "Help with hiring" | "Intros to anyone who has run customer support at a company selling into UK pharmacy — we're hiring our first support lead." |
| "Introductions to customers" | "Intros to heads of operations at UK homeware brands doing £5m to £30m. Happy to send a one-line forwardable." |
| "Advice on pricing" | "We're moving from £49/mo to £3,600/yr. If you've watched a portfolio company do a 6x increase, I'd like 20 minutes." |
| "Let us know if you can help" | "Anyone who knows a regulatory affairs consultant who would do two days a month on contract." |

Two asks maximum. Ten asks is zero asks, because it reads as a wish list rather than a request. Include a forwardable sentence for anything introduction-shaped — removing the work of composing the forward raises the response rate more than anything else you can do.

And close the loop. When an ask gets answered, say so in the next update, naming who helped. It costs one line, it is the reason the next ask gets answered, and it is the only visible reward most of these readers get.

## Cadence and distribution

**Monthly.** Quarterly is enough to be a formality and not enough to be a relationship. Weekly is too often to have anything to say.

**Same date every month.** Put it in the calendar as a recurring block. The date matters more than which date.

**Email, plain text, in the body.** Not an attachment, not a link to a dashboard, not a PDF. The reader is on a phone between two meetings.

**Who gets it:** every current investor; every advisor who has actually helped; and every investor who declined but asked to be kept posted.

That third group is the most undervalued asset in this process. An investor who said "too early" and receives eight months of updates showing exactly the progress they named has watched you do what you said. That is a materially warmer lead than any introduction, and it costs you one extra address on an email you were already sending.

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. The company and numbers are invented; no company described here exists.*

Here is a complete update for a bad month — 310 words, which is the length the whole thing should be.

> **Subject: Hallow — October update (runway 9 months)**
>
> Cash £412k · net burn £46k/mo · **runway 9 months**
>
> **Bad news first.** We lost our two largest accounts this month, both to the same cause: our weekly export breaks on files over 2,000 rows and both had grown past that. That is £4,100 of monthly recurring revenue, about 19% of the book. The fix shipped on the 22nd and is in production; I have offered both accounts three free months to come back and one has agreed to a call. I should have treated the row limit as a roadmap item in July when the first customer hit it, and I logged it as an edge case instead.
>
> **Metrics** (customer = paying account; MRR = contracted monthly recurring revenue, excluding one-off setup)
>
> - MRR: £17,600 (was £21,700)
> - Paying customers: 29 (was 31)
> - Gross monthly churn: 6.5% (was 0%)
>
> **Most important thing that happened.** Separately from the churn, our first two customers acquired through the regulatory-affairs job-posting trigger both converted inside three weeks, against a six-week average elsewhere. Small sample, but it is the first evidence that the trigger list converts materially faster, and we are shifting outbound entirely onto it in November.
>
> **Asks**
>
> 1. Intros to heads of operations at UK homeware or furniture brands doing £5m–£30m revenue. One-line forwardable below.
> 2. If anyone has watched a portfolio company come back from a top-two-account loss, I would like twenty minutes on what they did about the remaining concentration — our largest account is now 14% of MRR.
>
> Thanks to Priya for the intro to the Castle Hill team last month, which became our third-largest account.
>
> — P

What that update does: the runway is in the subject line, so a reader who opens nothing learns the most important fact. The worst news is in the first paragraph with a number, a fix, a date and an admission. Metric definitions are stated inline. The good news is labelled as a small sample rather than oversold. Both asks are actionable in five seconds, and one of them admits a vulnerability — account concentration — that the reader would have worked out anyway.

What it deliberately does not do: no explanation of the churn in terms of market conditions, no three-paragraph product roadmap, no screenshot, no attachment, no "excited to share."

## Checklist

- [ ] Recurring calendar block, same date monthly, 40 minutes.
- [ ] Runway in months in the subject line and the first line.
- [ ] Cash, net burn, months remaining — all three, every month.
- [ ] Two or three metrics, each with a definition stated in the same words as last month.
- [ ] Any definition change flagged explicitly, with prior months restated.
- [ ] The worst thing of the month in the first three lines when it is the main event.
- [ ] Bad news carries a number, an owner and a date.
- [ ] A "what went wrong" section present even in good months.
- [ ] One or two asks, each specific enough to act on in five seconds.
- [ ] A forwardable sentence included for any introduction ask.
- [ ] Last month's answered asks acknowledged by name.
- [ ] Under 500 words, plain text, in the email body.
- [ ] Sent to current investors, helpful advisors, and investors who declined but asked to be kept posted.
- [ ] Sent on the date, regardless of the month.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** You have not sent an update in a quarter.
**Cause:** The month was bad, skipping was free, and the cost of resuming rose each month.
**Fix:** Send one this week. Open with one sentence naming the gap and why, then the normal structure. Do not write an apology paragraph; the next eleven on-time updates are the apology.

**Symptom:** An investor learned something significant from someone other than you.
**Cause:** Omission, which reads as concealment whether or not it was.
**Fix:** Address it in the next update explicitly, including that they heard it elsewhere. Then be the first source for the next twelve months.

**Symptom:** Asks never get answered.
**Cause:** They are sentiments, not requests. The reader cannot tell if they are the right person.
**Fix:** Rewrite to name a role, a company size, a geography or a person. Add the forwardable line. Reduce to two.

**Symptom:** A number moved in a direction you cannot explain and an investor asked about it.
**Cause:** A definition drifted, usually innocently, while the label stayed the same.
**Fix:** One definitions line, fixed, in every update. Restate prior months when it changes and say that you have.

**Symptom:** Updates are long and nobody replies.
**Cause:** Length. A 1,200-word update is skimmed and a 300-word one is read.
**Fix:** Five sections, 500-word ceiling. Cut the roadmap first.

**Symptom:** Every update reads as relentlessly positive and you can feel it.
**Cause:** No "what went wrong" discipline, so the document became marketing.
**Fix:** The section is mandatory. If a month really had nothing, say that in one sentence — but check first, because a month with nothing is usually a month where nobody looked.

**Symptom:** During a raise, existing investors needed re-briefing from scratch.
**Cause:** No update history, so there is nothing to re-brief from.
**Fix:** Nothing fast, which is the point. Start now so the *next* raise is different. See [raising a first round](/run/fundraising/).
