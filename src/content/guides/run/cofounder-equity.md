## TL;DR

- Have the conversation **before the company exists**, while nobody has anything to lose by having it. The same conversation two years in is between people with stakes and divergent memories.
- Split by **what each person will do over the next four years**, not by what has happened so far. Past contribution is small and knowable. Future contribution is large and uncertain — and **vesting, not the headline split, is the instrument for the uncertain part**.
- An even split is sometimes right and is a **warning sign when it was chosen to avoid discomfort**. The avoided conversation does not disappear; it resurfaces as resentment once contributions visibly diverge.
- **Everyone vests, including you.** Four years, one-year cliff. Without it, a co-founder who leaves in month eight keeps their whole stake while you build value for them for six years.
- Write down **what happens when someone leaves** before anyone wants to. Good leaver, bad leaver, acceleration, buyback. Unarguable in advance; almost unagreeable once a departure is live.

## Who this is for

You are starting something with one or more other people, or you already have and never papered it properly. Possibly the split was agreed in a pub and nobody has mentioned it since.

Skip this if you are a solo founder with no co-founder in prospect — go to [first hires](/founder-playbook/run/first-hires/) instead, where the equity question is about employees. Skip it too if you have institutional investors and a lawyer on retainer; this is already handled and your version of the question is governance.

## The problem

Founder equity is decided at the worst possible moment by the worst possible method, and both are predictable.

**The moment** is the beginning, when there is nothing to divide, everyone is enthusiastic, and raising the question feels like a vote of no confidence in the relationship. So it gets settled fast — often evenly, often verbally — and the speed is experienced as a sign of alignment rather than as the avoidance it usually is.

**The method** is a negotiation about the past. Who had the idea, who has been working on it longer, who quit their job first. All of that is real and almost all of it is small, because the company's value will be created by what happens over the next four to seven years, not by the three months before incorporation. Dividing on the basis of the past is dividing on the basis of the smallest available input.

The research most often cited on this is Noam Wasserman's, drawn from a dataset of thousands of founders and set out in *The Founder's Dilemmas* (Princeton University Press, 2012). His argument has two parts worth separating. The first is that founding teams tend to split equity very early and treat the result as fixed. The second, more useful, is that a fast and even split frequently indicates that the hard conversation about differing contributions was skipped — and that **dynamic arrangements, principally vesting, are the mechanism that limits the cost when the premise of the original split stops being true.**

One honest note on the citation: specific percentages from that work circulate widely and the figures restated in secondary summaries do not consistently agree with each other, so none is quoted here. The argument is what transfers.

Then there is the failure that is purely administrative and does more damage than any disagreement about percentages: **nothing was written down**. Not the split, not vesting, not the assignment of intellectual property from each founder to the company. That last one is the quiet catastrophe. A founder who wrote code before incorporation and never assigned it owns it personally. Diligence finds this, every time, and it is discovered at the moment you have least leverage — the same dynamic [raising a first round](/founder-playbook/run/fundraising/) describes about starting a raise late.

## What the equity is actually paying for

Separate three things that get collapsed into one number.

**What has already happened.** The idea, prior work, early capital, a prototype. Real, knowable, and small relative to what comes next. The correct way to handle it is a modest adjustment to the split, or a small number of shares that vest immediately — not a large permanent premium.

**What each person will do over four years.** This is almost all of the value and almost all of the uncertainty. You cannot know it now. That is precisely why the instrument for it is **vesting**, which converts a promise about the future into something earned as the future happens.

**The role each person holds.** Who is accountable for what. This belongs in a founder agreement rather than in the percentage, and writing it down surfaces disagreements that the percentage conversation hides — two people who both think they are the chief executive will agree on 50/50 very easily.

The practical consequence: **spend less argument on the split and more on the vesting and the departure terms.** Founders do the opposite, because the percentage is the part that feels like it is about fairness. The departure terms are the part that determines what actually happens.

## The split

A method rather than a formula.

**Each person writes, separately, what they will own for the next four years.** Not a job title — the specific areas they are accountable for and the specific things they will do in the first year. Then compare. Disagreement here is the real finding, and it is much cheaper to discover now.

**Then agree a split and state the reason out loud.** The test is whether you can each say the number with a reason attached, in one sentence, without flinching. "We're 55/45 because you're full-time from today and I'm part-time until March" is a sentence. "It's 50/50 because we're partners" is a sentiment, and sentiments do not survive the first time contributions diverge visibly.

**Even splits are fine when they are chosen rather than defaulted to.** Two people doing comparable work at comparable risk, both full-time, both committed — an even split is the honest answer and you should take it. The question is never the number; it is whether you arrived at it or retreated to it.

**Revisit the inputs, not the agreement.** If someone's situation changes materially before you incorporate — one person stays employed for six months, say — adjust then. After incorporation, the agreement is the agreement, and renegotiating it is a far worse process than vesting handles automatically.

## Vesting

The single most important mechanism in this guide, and the one founders most often skip because it feels like distrust.

**The standard shape:** four years, one-year cliff. Nothing vests for twelve months. At twelve months, a quarter vests at once. Then monthly for the remaining three years. **Every founder, including whoever is writing the document.**

**Why it protects you rather than constraining you.** Imagine your co-founder leaves at month eight with no vesting. They own their full stake. You spend the next six years building a company in which a person who worked for eight months holds a large static share, and every investor you meet asks about it, and the honest answer is damaging. With vesting and a one-year cliff, they leave with nothing and the equity returns to the company — which is the outcome every remaining founder wants and no departing founder negotiates for in advance.

**Acceleration.** What happens on an acquisition. "Single trigger" vests on the sale. "Double trigger" vests only if the company is sold *and* the person is terminated afterwards. Double trigger is the common and more defensible arrangement, because an acquirer buying a team does not want that team's incentives to vanish at closing.

**Good leaver and bad leaver.** Define both, in writing, now. A good leaver — illness, agreed departure — typically keeps what has vested. A bad leaver — gross misconduct, competing — may lose more. These definitions are easy to agree while nobody is leaving and nearly impossible once someone is.

**A buyback right** lets the company repurchase vested shares from a departing founder at a defined price. It is worth discussing; it is also the term most likely to feel aggressive, so it is worth discussing early for exactly that reason.

## The documents

Four things, and a lawyer who does this routinely. The whole set is cheap relative to any one of the problems it prevents.

| Document | What it does | Why it is not optional |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Founder agreement** | Roles, decision rights, time commitment, what happens on disagreement or departure. | It is the written record of what you agreed while you still remembered it the same way. |
| **Share subscription with vesting** | Issues the shares and attaches the vesting, cliff, acceleration and leaver terms. | A verbal vesting agreement is not vesting. |
| **IP assignment from every founder** | Transfers everything each founder created for the company to the company. | Without it, a founder personally owns work the company depends on. Diligence finds this every time. |
| **Cap table** | One sheet: who owns what, including every instrument issued. | Kept current, it is four minutes a month. Reconstructed later, it is a week and an unpleasant surprise. See [raising a first round](/founder-playbook/run/fundraising/). |

On the cap table specifically: keep every convertible instrument on it with its cap and its implied percentage, updated the day it is signed. Founders who do not routinely cannot say what they will own after conversion, and find out during a priced round.

## When it goes wrong

**One founder is contributing far less than the split implies.** The most common serious founder problem, and almost always allowed to run for months because the conversation is unbearable. Have it directly, early, against what was written down in the roles document. Vesting is what makes the outcome survivable: a departure at month fourteen with a four-year schedule returns three quarters of the stake to the company.

**One founder wants out.** Better than the previous case, because it is explicit. Follow the leaver terms. Be generous within them and do not renegotiate outside them — the terms exist precisely so this conversation is short.

**You never papered it and now you are raising.** Fix it before the raise, not during. Retroactive vesting is possible and requires everyone to agree while each person understands exactly what they are giving up, which is harder than agreeing it at the start and far easier than explaining its absence to an investor.

**A departed founder holds a large stake.** No clean fix. Options: buy them out, if you can afford it and they will sell; or disclose it clearly and early in every raise. Investors dislike surprises more than they dislike a messy history.

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. Numbers are invented to be plausible; no company described here exists.*

Two founders. A, an engineer, has been building a prototype evenings and weekends for five months and had the original idea. B, who will do sales and operations, joins full-time from the start; A stays employed for four more months to cover their mortgage.

**The default they nearly took.** 50/50, agreed in twenty minutes, nothing written. B suggests writing it down; A finds this slightly awkward and agrees anyway.

**What the conversation surfaced.** They each write what they will own for four years. A's list: the product, engineering hiring, technical architecture. B's list: sales, fundraising, operations, hiring — **and** product direction. The overlap on product direction is a genuine disagreement neither knew they had, and it takes two evenings to resolve. The resolution: A owns what gets built, B owns what customers are told, and disagreements about what to build go to whoever can cite a customer conversation. That sentence is worth more than the percentage.

**The split they agreed.** 52/48 in A's favour, with a written reason: A contributed five months of prototype work and carries the technical risk; B is full-time four months earlier. Both can say the number with the reason, which was the test.

They deliberately spend ten minutes on this and three hours on what follows.

**Vesting.** Four years, one-year cliff, both founders. Double-trigger acceleration on acquisition. A's five months of prior work recognised as 4% vesting immediately rather than as a bigger headline split — which is the move that let the split stay close to even without pretending the prototype did not exist.

Good leaver and bad leaver defined. Company buyback right on vested shares at fair value, which B raises and A initially finds uncomfortable; they include it.

**The documents.** A lawyer who does this weekly. Founder agreement, share subscription with vesting, IP assignment from both. The IP assignment catches something: A's prototype used a library under a licence requiring attribution, and two files were copied from a previous employer's internal tooling. The second is a real problem, caught at a stage where rewriting two files costs a weekend. Found in diligence two years later it would have cost a deal.

**Month nineteen.** B's circumstances change and they leave, amicably. Nineteen months of a 48-month schedule: roughly 40% of their 48% has vested, about 19% of the company. The remaining 29 points return to the company. B keeps the 19%, which both consider fair for nineteen months of full-time work. The conversation takes one afternoon, because every term was already written.

A then raises a seed round. The investor asks about B, as every investor does. The answer — vesting schedule, 19% held, departure amicable, terms in the founder agreement — takes thirty seconds and closes the topic. Without vesting the answer would have been that a founder who worked nineteen months holds 48%, and it would not have closed the topic.

**What they got wrong.** They did not update the cap table when they issued two small advisor grants in year one, and reconstructed it during the raise from email. It took two days and produced one discrepancy they had to explain. Four minutes a month would have prevented it.

## Checklist

Before incorporating:

- [ ] Each founder has written, separately, what they will own for four years.
- [ ] Lists compared and overlaps resolved in writing.
- [ ] A split agreed that each person can state with a reason, in one sentence, without flinching.
- [ ] Past contribution handled as a modest adjustment or immediately-vesting shares, not a permanent premium.

Vesting and terms:

- [ ] Four years with a one-year cliff, for **every** founder.
- [ ] Acceleration decided — double trigger unless there is a specific reason otherwise.
- [ ] Good leaver and bad leaver defined in writing.
- [ ] Company buyback right on vested shares discussed explicitly, however uncomfortable.

Documents:

- [ ] Founder agreement covering roles, decision rights, time commitment, departure.
- [ ] Share subscription agreement carrying the vesting terms.
- [ ] IP assignment signed by every founder, covering pre-incorporation work.
- [ ] Any third-party code, licences or previous-employer material audited before the assignment.
- [ ] A lawyer who does founder documents routinely, not a generalist.

Ongoing:

- [ ] Cap table updated the day anything is issued, with every convertible instrument and its implied percentage.
- [ ] Roles document revisited annually, since what people actually own drifts.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** The split was agreed in minutes and nobody has mentioned it since.
**Cause:** Avoidance read as alignment.
**Fix:** Have the conversation now, before incorporation if possible. It is strictly cheaper today than at any future point.

**Symptom:** One founder is visibly contributing less and nobody has said so.
**Cause:** The conversation is unbearable, so it is deferred while resentment accrues.
**Fix:** Raise it against the written roles document rather than as a judgement about effort. Vesting is what makes the outcome survivable.

**Symptom:** A founder left and kept everything.
**Cause:** No vesting.
**Fix:** Nothing retroactive. Disclose it early in every raise; a surprise costs more than the fact.

**Symptom:** Diligence found unassigned intellectual property.
**Cause:** No IP assignment at incorporation.
**Fix:** Get it signed immediately, which requires the founder's cooperation — easy while relations are good and not guaranteed otherwise.

**Symptom:** You cannot say what you own after all instruments convert.
**Cause:** Cap table not maintained.
**Fix:** One sheet, every instrument, implied percentage, updated on signature. [Raising a first round](/founder-playbook/run/fundraising/) has the arithmetic.

**Symptom:** Both founders believe they are accountable for the same decisions.
**Cause:** Roles were never written, and an even split concealed it.
**Fix:** Write what each owns and who decides when you disagree. The sentence matters more than the percentage.

- A note on what is **not** cited: no benchmark split, vesting variation or founder-equity distribution appears here. The datasets that would support one are either proprietary cap-table products that update continuously and block automated access, or self-selected survey samples, and a founder split is in any case a decision about two or three specific people rather than a draw from a distribution.
