## TL;DR

- Hire against a **trigger**, not a role name. The trigger is a queue you cannot clear, a skill the company genuinely lacks, or a function with no owner. A title on an imagined org chart is not a reason.
- **The botched hire is the early salesperson.** If you cannot write the motion on one page — target, opener, three objections, three answers — your new hire is doing discovery on commission, which almost nobody does well. See [founder-led sales](/sell/).
- Replace the interview with a **paid work sample**. Three to six hours of the real work, judged on output. Unstructured interviews mostly measure whether you enjoy the conversation.
- There is **no reliable public benchmark** for early-stage equity. Be internally consistent instead: a written band structure applied to everyone, with strike price, vesting and the preference stack stated plainly.
- Write **what good looks like at 30, 60 and 90 days** and give it to them on day one. Firing fast is only defensible if the standard was stated; otherwise the failure is yours.

## Who this is for

You are making your first few hires, or you have made one or two and at least one is not going well. You do not have a recruiter, a process, or any calibration about what good looks like for a role you have never done.

Skip this if you have a head of people and a structured loop. Skip it too if your question is whether you can avoid hiring by building differently, which is [shipping without engineers](/build/shipping-without-engineers/).

## The problem

Early hires fail for one of two reasons, and they look identical from the outside.

**The hire was made to escape discomfort.** Something is unpleasant — support email, outbound, operations, the part of the product you hate — and hiring promises to remove it. The promise is partly true and the omission is large: you have not removed the work, you have added the work of managing someone doing it, and at five people managing is not a role anyone has spare capacity for. The tell is a job description that is mostly a list of things the founder stopped wanting to do.

**The hire was made against an imagined company.** You picture the company at forty people, see a VP of something in that picture, and hire toward it. The person arrives to find no team, no process and a founder who is still doing the job. Senior people hired into companies that are not yet senior-shaped leave within a year, and they leave having cost you more than their salary, because a departure at seven people is a visible event that everyone interprets.

Both are versions of the same error: hiring against a **plan** rather than against a **constraint**.

The cost is also systematically underestimated, in a way worth being specific about. The salary is the small part. There is the recruiting cost — SHRM's benchmarking, [reported in April 2022](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/real-costs-recruitment), put average cost per hire at "nearly $4,700", though SHRM's article does not disclose the sample or methodology behind that figure, and a mean in a distribution with agency-fee outliers sits well above the typical case. Then onboarding, which consumes founder time at exactly the moment you hired to free it. Then, if it fails, the cost of a departure in a small team, which is the part nobody budgets: a leaver at seven people is a signal everyone reads, and the reading is usually worse than the truth.

And the failure is quiet rather than dramatic. A bad early hire does not blow up. They are pleasant, busy, and produce work that is nearly right, and you spend four months explaining the gap to yourself before acting on it.

## The order, as triggers

Not a sequence of titles. A set of conditions, in the rough order they tend to arrive.

| Trigger | Hire | Why this and not the senior version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A founder is spending more than a day a week operating software they cannot safely change. | A contract or full-time engineer, mid-level. | You need someone to make changes safely, not to lead a team that does not exist. |
| Support or delivery has a queue the founders cannot clear, and the questions are mostly repeats. | A support or operations person, junior to mid. | Write the three missing documents first — frequently half the queue disappears and the role shrinks to something real. |
| You can write the sales motion on one page and someone else has won a deal with it. | A first salesperson, closer than leader. | Before that test passes, nothing about this hire works. It is the subject of the next section. |
| A function is genuinely load-bearing and has no owner — finance, compliance, infrastructure. | A specialist, possibly fractional. | Fractional is underused. A day a week of someone genuinely senior beats a full-time person who is learning. |
| Two or more people do the same function and are making conflicting decisions. | A lead for that function. | This is the real trigger for seniority: coordination cost, not headcount ambition. |

Two things to notice.

**Nothing on that list is a "head of" until the last row.** Seniority is a response to coordination cost. Hiring it before the coordination cost exists means paying for judgement you will overrule, which is the shape of the senior-hire failure.

**Fractional deserves more consideration than it gets.** For finance, compliance, security, data and regulatory work, a genuinely senior person for a day a week often outperforms a full-time mid-level hire, and the commitment is reversible. It is the hiring equivalent of the rule in [the tool stack](/build/): prefer the reversible option until you can name why it is insufficient.

## The one founders botch

Hiring a salesperson too early. It is the most expensive avoidable mistake at this stage and it happens for an understandable reason: selling is the most uncomfortable part of the job and the one whose absence is most visible in the numbers.

The precondition is the describability test from [founder-led sales](/sell/). You can write, on one page: who to contact and why they have the problem; what to say first; the three objections in order of frequency; the answer to each; the price and when to say it; and what qualified means specifically enough that someone else could apply it. Then you hand the page to one person and **they win a deal you were not in**.

If that test has not passed, the hire does not work, and the mechanism is worth understanding rather than just accepting. A salesperson's skill is executing a known motion efficiently. Discovery — finding out who the buyer is, what language works, which objections are real — is a different skill, it is slower, and it is nearly impossible to do while carrying a quota, because the quota rewards closing whatever is in front of you rather than learning what should be. So you get activity, a pipeline of poorly qualified deals, and a quarter later a conclusion that the hire was wrong when the actual error was in the brief.

It also costs you twice. You pay the salary and the ramp, and you forgo the quarter in which you would have finished discovery yourself.

The honest version of the temptation: founders hire salespeople to stop having to sell. If that is the reason, the hire will not fix it — you will be doing the same calls plus managing someone.

A runner-up, worth naming: the **early senior marketing hire**, made because growth feels like a function rather than a consequence. At pre-product-market-fit the thing that moves is usually the words on the site and who you are talking to, both of which are founder work and both of which are covered by [discovery calls](/sell/discovery-and-demos/) and [outbound](/sell/outbound/).

## Interviewing with no process

You have never hired, have no calibration, and will be making a decision with real consequences on roughly three hours of conversation. Unstructured interviews mostly measure whether you enjoy talking to someone, which is not the job.

Four things that work.

**A paid work sample.** Three to six hours of the actual work, paid at a fair rate. For an engineer, a small real change in a scratch copy of the codebase. For a support hire, answering ten real past tickets. For a salesperson, a cold email to a named prospect from your list plus a fifteen-minute mock first call where you play a buyer using your own real objections. Judge the output, not the enthusiasm.

This is the single highest-signal step available to a first-time hirer, and it is also a filter in both directions: a candidate who will not do a paid sample is telling you something, and a candidate who does one well has shown you the thing you are buying.

**A reference conversation that permits nuance.** "Would you hire them again?" produces yes. Ask instead: *what would you have them do again, and what would you not?* And: *what did they need from you to do their best work?* The second question is the most useful one in hiring — it tells you whether you can provide that, and at five people you frequently cannot.

Ask for a former manager, not a peer, and ask the candidate to make the introduction. The friction in that request is itself informative.

**One question about the last time they did the thing.** Not hypothetical. "Walk me through the last time you had to do X — what did you actually do, in order." Hypothetical answers measure articulacy; recalled specifics are much harder to manufacture, and the detail density tells you whether they did it or watched it.

**Written expectations before the offer.** What good looks like at 30, 60 and 90 days, in the offer or the first-day email. This is the single thing that makes a fast exit defensible later, and writing it also catches the case where *you* cannot say what success would look like — which is a reason not to hire yet, discovered for free.

## Pay and equity with no data

Two honest problems: you cannot verify the market, and you have very little money.

On benchmarks, the position is similar to [pricing](/sell/pricing/). There is no reliable public dataset for what a first hire at a seven-person pre-Series-A company should receive, and most circulating figures are restatements. The credible sources are cap-table and payroll aggregators publishing from their own data, they update continuously, and they generally do not permit automated access — so look them up yourself at the time you need them rather than trusting a number in an article, including this one.

What you can control is **internal consistency**, which matters more than being slightly off an unverifiable market rate.

Before the first offer, write down a simple structure: two or three salary bands with a rough seniority definition, and an equity range per band. Apply it to everyone. Early hires talk to each other, and an inconsistency between hire two and hire four is discovered, remembered, and expensive in a way that being 10% below market is not.

When you make an equity offer, state plainly: the number of options, the total shares outstanding so the percentage is computable, the strike price, the vesting schedule and cliff, what happens on a sale, and — the one most founders omit — that there is a **preference stack ahead of common shares** and roughly what it is. A candidate who later discovers that their options were worth nothing in a modest exit, and that nobody mentioned the preference, has learned something about you rather than about equity. The mechanics are in [raising a first round](/run/fundraising/).

And say what the offer is not: it is a lottery ticket with a strike price, not deferred compensation. Candidates who need certainty should take the salary and that is a legitimate choice.

## Firing fast, properly

"Hire slow, fire fast" is correct and usually quoted by people skipping the precondition.

The precondition is that the person was **told what success looked like, in writing, on day one**. If they were not, the failure is yours, and firing them for missing an unstated standard is both unfair and uninformative — you learn nothing you can apply to the next hire.

Given that, act inside the first quarter. The cost of waiting is paid immediately by the other four people, who can all see it and are all quietly recalculating what the standard is here. And nobody who was going to work out is ambiguous at ninety days.

Mechanically: a direct conversation at thirty days naming the specific gap against the written expectations, with what would need to change; a second at sixty; a decision at ninety. Severance beyond the legal minimum if you can afford it, because a small company's reputation is made of how people describe leaving it.

The thing to resist is the four-month explanation period, where you are privately building a case while publicly behaving normally. It is worse for them than an honest thirty-day conversation and it is corrosive to you.

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. Numbers are invented to be plausible; no company described here exists.*

Two founders, £44,000 of monthly recurring revenue, five people including themselves, ten months of runway. Growth has flattened for two months. They decide to hire a salesperson, reasoning that selling is the bottleneck and neither of them enjoys it.

**The test they nearly skipped.** One founder tries to write the one-page motion. She gets the target right — operations leads at UK distributors with 20 to 200 staff — and stalls on the objections, because the three she writes down are not actually the three she hears; they are the three she has good answers to. Going back through eleven months of call notes, the real top objection is "we'd need our warehouse team to adopt it," which she has never had a good answer for and has been handling by changing the subject.

So the motion is not describable. They do not hire the salesperson.

**What they hire instead.** The actual queue they can name: one founder is spending roughly eleven hours a week on customer onboarding, each one manual, each one slightly different. That is a trigger — a queue, repeating, with the founders unable to clear it.

They hire an operations person, mid-level, £38,000. Before writing the job description they do the thing the table above recommends: write the three documents the onboarding depends on. Two of the eleven weekly hours disappear immediately, and — more usefully — writing them reveals that onboarding varies because of one upstream product gap, not because customers differ.

**The hiring process.** 34 applicants, 6 phone screens, 3 paid work samples at £150 each. The sample is: here are three real customer data files and the onboarding checklist, produce the configuration for each and tell us what you would ask the customer. One candidate produces clean output and a list of four questions, two of which the founders had never thought to ask. She gets the offer.

References: the "what did they need from you to do their best work" question produces "she needs to know why a thing matters, not just what to do — she'll push back if the reason is thin." The founders discuss whether they can provide that at five people and conclude yes, and that it is probably good for them.

Written expectations go out with the offer: by 30 days, running onboarding with a founder observing; by 60, running it alone; by 90, the checklist rewritten in her own words and two of the manual steps eliminated.

**Six months later.** She is doing all onboarding, has removed four manual steps, and has started answering support. The founder's eleven hours is down to about one. Those reclaimed hours went into founder selling — and the warehouse-adoption objection now has a real answer, because the operations hire built a two-page rollout guide for warehouse teams, which turned out to be the missing piece. The motion becomes describable in month seven.

**The salesperson, hired in month eight**, closes a deal in week three that neither founder touched.

**What they got wrong.** The band structure was written after the second hire rather than before, and the operations hire's equity grant was set by feel. Hire three, two months later, negotiated harder and got more equity for a comparable role. They found out when the two compared notes, and fixing it cost a top-up grant, a difficult conversation, and some trust that took a while to come back. Writing the bands first costs an hour.

## Checklist

Before opening a role:

- [ ] The trigger written in one sentence: the queue, the missing skill, or the unowned function.
- [ ] Honest answer to whether this is a constraint or a discomfort.
- [ ] For a salesperson: the one-page motion written **and** validated by someone else winning a deal I was not in.
- [ ] Documents written first, where the queue is mostly repeated questions. Re-scope the role afterwards.
- [ ] Fractional or contract considered, and a reason recorded if rejected.
- [ ] Band structure written: salary bands with seniority definitions and an equity range each, applied to everyone.
- [ ] What good looks like at 30, 60 and 90 days, written down. If I cannot write it, I am not ready to hire.

Process:

- [ ] A paid work sample of three to six hours of the real work, judged on output.
- [ ] A reference conversation with a former manager, asking what they would have them do again, what they would not, and what support they needed.
- [ ] At least one "walk me through the last time you did this" question, answered with specifics.
- [ ] Equity stated completely: option count, total shares outstanding, strike price, vesting and cliff, and the existence and rough size of the preference stack.
- [ ] Expectations sent in writing with the offer, not after the start date.

After:

- [ ] A specific conversation against the written expectations at 30 and 60 days.
- [ ] A decision at 90 days.
- [ ] The hire mentioned in the next [investor update](/run/investor-updates/), including if it went badly.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** A salesperson has been in seat a quarter with a full pipeline and no closes.
**Cause:** The motion was not describable, so they are doing discovery on commission.
**Fix:** Take selling back for a quarter and finish discovery. Keep them on inbound or expansion meanwhile, or part ways honestly — it was the brief, not the person.

**Symptom:** A senior hire is frustrated and under-occupied.
**Cause:** Hired against the imagined company. There is no team, no process, and you are still doing the job.
**Fix:** Either give them real ownership including the right to overrule you, or acknowledge the mismatch early. Nothing in between works.

**Symptom:** You hired to free up time and have less of it.
**Cause:** Management is work, and at five people nobody had spare capacity for it.
**Fix:** Front-load it — heavy for six weeks, then light. And write the documents, because undocumented work cannot be handed over, only supervised.

**Symptom:** Four months in you are still explaining a performance gap to yourself.
**Cause:** No written standard, so there is nothing to measure against and no conversation to have.
**Fix:** Write the standard now, share it, give thirty days. Future hires get it on day one.

**Symptom:** Two early hires discovered their equity differs for comparable roles.
**Cause:** No band structure. Offers were made by feel in the order they occurred.
**Fix:** Write the bands, correct the inequity rather than defending it, and expect the trust recovery to take longer than the correction.

**Symptom:** A departed employee describes the company badly and it is reaching candidates.
**Cause:** Usually how the exit was handled rather than the exit itself.
**Fix:** Direct conversations against stated expectations, decisions inside a quarter, severance beyond the minimum where affordable.

**Symptom:** The job description reads as a list of things the founders no longer want to do.
**Cause:** Hiring to escape discomfort.
**Fix:** Rewrite it as the trigger plus the outcome owned. If that rewrite is not possible, the role is not real yet.
