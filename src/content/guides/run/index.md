## TL;DR

- The job has **four irreducible duties**: set direction, hold the money, hire and fire the top layer, and be the last decision. Everything else is delegable in principle. Most founders spend most of their week outside all four.
- The diagnosis is always a **calendar audit**, backwards. Sort last month's recorded hours against the four duties. The gap between the week you intended and the week you kept is the entire finding.
- Delegate by **consequence, not competence**. Hand over anything recoverable where the information is local to the person doing it. Someone being worse at it this quarter is the price of the transfer, not an argument against it.
- Sort decisions by **reversibility**. Fast and alone when they can be undone; slow and consultative only when they cannot. Treating everything as weighty is the most common way a founder becomes the bottleneck on a working company.
- Every founder has **one activity they hide in** — the thing they are good at that postpones something harder. Naming yours is most of the fix.

## Who this is for

You have employees. Decisions route to you that you do not think should. You end weeks unable to say what you did, and you suspect that the things you were busy with were not the things that mattered. Maybe you also notice you are doing a version of your old job, well, while the actual job goes unattended.

Skip this if you are still pre-employees — your job is almost entirely product and selling, and [founder-led sales](/founder-playbook/sell/) is the better use of the hour. Skip it too if your question is specifically who to hire next, which is [first hires](/founder-playbook/run/first-hires/).

## The problem

The CEO job is the only one in the company with no job description, and that is not an oversight — it is structural. Every other role is defined by what it excludes. Yours is defined as the residual: whatever is both important and unowned. Which means it changes shape every quarter and nobody will tell you when it has.

Three failure modes follow, and nearly every founder recognises at least one.

**You do your old job, well.** An engineer founder writes code. A designer founder designs. A salesperson founder sells past the point of usefulness. This is the comfortable failure because the work is real, you are good at it, and it visibly progresses. It is also the expensive one, because you are doing a job someone else could do while the job only you can do goes undone.

**You become the bottleneck.** You are the most informed person, so routing decisions through you looks efficient. Then eleven things wait on you, and the company's throughput becomes your inbox's throughput. The tell is not that you feel busy. It is that work stops when you are away.

**You do whatever is loudest.** With no job description, urgency becomes the filter. The quarter fills with escalations, each legitimately urgent, and at the end of it nothing you chose in advance has happened.

What makes all three hard to see is that they feel like working hard, and they are. The missing information is not effort. It is **which hours served a duty nobody else could hold**.

It is worth being honest about the evidence here, because this is a topic where confident numbers circulate freely. The one rigorous study of how chief executives actually spend time is Michael Porter and Nitin Nohria's, [published in Harvard Business Review in July–August 2018](https://hbr.org/2018/07/how-ceos-manage-time). They tracked 27 chief executives around the clock for 13 weeks each, gathering roughly **60,000 hours** of data. They found those leaders worked **9.7 hours per weekday**, conducted business on **79% of weekend days** at an average of 3.9 hours, and on **70% of vacation days** at 2.4 hours. Of work time, **61%** was spent in face-to-face interaction, **15%** on phone and written correspondence, and **24%** on electronic interaction.

And the sample is large-company chief executives, not founders. Their constraints — a board, an existing strategy, a management layer, a brand — are almost the inverse of yours. So do not copy the allocation. What transfers is the shape of the finding: the job is overwhelmingly **interaction**, email is what the authors call a "dangerous time sink," and the only real lever a chief executive has over their own time is the agenda they set in advance. Those three observations survive the change of scale. The percentages do not.

## The four duties

| Duty | What it means concretely | Why only you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direction** | What the company is doing this year and, crucially, what it has decided not to do. Written down, repeated until it is boring. | It is the one statement everyone else's decisions are made against. Delegated, it fragments into several directions nobody notices are different. |
| **Money** | How much there is, how long it lasts, what it is being spent on, and when the next raise starts. See [raising a first round](/founder-playbook/run/fundraising/). | Runway is the constraint every other decision sits inside. A founder who does not know the number to the week cannot make any other decision correctly. |
| **The top layer** | Hiring, and firing, the people who themselves own functions. | Each one changes what the company is capable of and what it is like to work there. Mistakes here compound through everyone they hire. [First hires](/founder-playbook/run/first-hires/) covers the decisions. |
| **The last decision** | The call on things that are genuinely contested or genuinely irreversible. | Someone has to be last, or decisions circulate. This is the duty most often confused with "all decisions." |

Early, there is a fifth, and pretending otherwise causes real damage: **you sell**. Until the motion can be written on one page and handed over — the test in [founder-led sales](/founder-playbook/sell/) — selling is a direction-setting activity wearing a sales hat, because it is where you find out what the company should be doing.

Everything else — product detail, hiring below the top layer, operations, support, the actual code, the actual designs, most of the recruiting process, nearly all meetings you did not call — is delegable in principle. "In principle" is doing real work in that sentence: you may be doing them because nobody else exists yet, which is fine and temporary. The problem is doing them because they are comfortable, which is neither.

## The audit

The diagnosis is backwards-looking, because intentions are unreliable and the calendar is not.

Take last month. Every hour with a block on it. Sort each into: direction, money, top layer, last decision, selling, or **none of the above**. Be strict — a meeting you attended because you were invited is "none of the above" unless it decided something only you could decide.

Then total the last column. For most founders with a team it is between half and three quarters of recorded time. That number is the finding. It is not an indictment: some of those hours are genuinely necessary, some are the price of a small company, and some are you keeping a function alive that has no owner yet. But you now know the size of the thing, and you cannot make decisions about an unmeasured quantity.

Two follow-up questions, which is where the audit earns its keep.

**What did you do most of?** Whatever it is, it is probably your old job or your refuge. Both look like work. Neither is the job.

**What did you not do at all?** Usually direction and money, in that order. Direction goes unattended because it has no deadline. Money goes unattended because the number is uncomfortable to look at, right up until it is urgent, at which point your options have narrowed.

## Delegation, properly

Founders delegate on the wrong axis. The usual question is "is this person better at it than me?" — which, for a founder who has been doing everything, is almost always no, so almost nothing transfers.

Ask two different questions.

**Is the consequence recoverable?** If the worst realistic outcome is a bad week, a lost deal, or an embarrassing email, delegate it. The cost of their mistakes is the price of the transfer, and it is cheaper than your continued involvement. If the worst outcome is a lawsuit, a breach, a departure, or a commitment lasting years, keep it for now.

**Where does the information live?** If the person doing the work sees the relevant facts first-hand and you see a summary, they should decide — your involvement adds a lossy translation step and a queue. If you hold context nobody else has, keep it until you have transferred the context, which is a separate task with its own cost and worth doing explicitly.

What this gives you is a sequence rather than a binary. Delegate recoverable work with local information **today**, accepting worse output for a quarter. Transfer context deliberately for the rest. Keep the irreversible.

And the part founders dislike hearing: **the output will get worse before it gets better**, and the dip is not evidence of a bad decision. If you reclaim the task at the first bad week, you have taught everyone that delegation is provisional, which costs you the next ten attempts.

## Decisions

The second lever, and the one with the fastest payoff. Sort every decision by whether it can be undone.

**Reversible.** Most decisions. A price experiment, a tool, a landing page, a process, a job posting's wording, a meeting's format. These should be made **fast, by one person, often not you**. The cost of being wrong is a reversal; the cost of deliberating is a week of several people's attention plus the delay.

**Hard to reverse.** Hiring and firing, raising money and its terms, a public pricing change, a platform or architecture commitment, a partnership with exclusivity, anything with a multi-year signature. These deserve slowness, dissent, and a written rationale naming what would make you revisit it.

The failure is treating everything as the second kind. It feels responsible. What it produces is a company where nothing moves without you, and — worse — where the genuinely irreversible decisions get the same twenty minutes as the reversible ones, because everything is equally crowded.

A cheap practice that works: when you make a hard-to-reverse call, write three sentences. What you decided, what you believe that the alternatives did not, and what evidence would change your mind. Takes two minutes. Six months later it is the only record of why, and it is the difference between learning from the decision and relitigating it.

## What only you can do

Worth stating positively, because the rest of this guide is subtractive.

**Say the direction out loud, repeatedly, past the point of boredom.** You will tire of it long before the company has absorbed it. The signal that it has landed is hearing someone else use your sentence in a decision you were not in.

**Know the money.** Runway to the week. Not a spreadsheet someone maintains — the number, in your head, now. Every other decision is made inside that constraint whether or not you are aware of it.

**Decide the contested things.** Not all things. The ones that are genuinely contested, where reasonable people have looked at the same facts and disagree. That is the residue after everything else is delegated, and it is the actual job.

**Keep a direct line to customers.** Permanently, at low volume. A few calls a month after you have hired sellers. Not for the revenue — to keep your own perception unfiltered. A founder whose only information about the market arrives through a summary loses the ability to notice it changing, and that loss is invisible until it is expensive.

**Tell the truth to the people who funded you.** Covered in [investor updates](/founder-playbook/run/investor-updates/), and it belongs on this list because it is unpleasant and delegable-looking and neither.

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. Numbers are invented to be plausible; no company described here exists.*

A technical founder, eleven people, roughly $900,000 of annual recurring revenue, fourteen months of runway. He feels behind constantly and cannot say what he is behind on.

**The audit.** Last month, 142 recorded hours.

- Direction: 3 hours. One offsite session that did not conclude.
- Money: 2 hours. One call with the accountant.
- Top layer: 9 hours. Interviews for a head of customer success.
- Last decision: 11 hours. Mostly genuine.
- Selling: 14 hours. Six calls, two closes.
- **None of the above: 103 hours.** Of which roughly 40 were code review and pair programming, 22 were standups and sprint ceremonies he did not run, 18 were support escalations, and the remainder was recruiting admin and scheduling.

Forty hours of code review is the headline. He is a good engineer and it is the thing he retreats into. It is also, by his own assessment, work his two senior engineers could do — he keeps it because a bad merge would be his fault and because reviewing code is the only part of his week with a visible finish line.

**The changes, over six weeks.**

*Code review.* Handed to the two senior engineers, with a written rule: anything touching billing or authentication still comes to him. That is the reversibility test applied honestly — most of it is recoverable, two areas are not. Reclaims about 32 hours a month. Quality drops noticeably for three weeks; two regressions ship. He does not take it back. By week six the rate is better than when he was doing it, because two reviewers catch more than one.

*Ceremonies.* Stops attending standups entirely. Asks for a written summary of blockers twice a week. 22 hours to about 2. The initial objection is that he will lose visibility; what he actually loses is visibility into detail he was not acting on.

*Support escalations.* These are the interesting ones. He reads a month of them and finds 14 of 19 were one of three known issues with no written answer anywhere. Writing the three answers takes an afternoon and removes most of the category. The remaining five were genuinely novel and genuinely needed him.

*Direction.* Uses four of the reclaimed hours to write a one-page statement of what the company is doing this year and three things it is explicitly not doing. Reads it at the all-hands. Nobody reacts much. Eight weeks later he hears a salesperson decline a deal using his third sentence, in a conversation he was not part of, which is the only evidence that direction has landed.

*Money.* Books thirty minutes every Monday with the spreadsheet open. Within three weeks he knows runway to the week and finds one surprise: a contract auto-renewing in February at nearly double, which he cancels — the specific failure mode [the tool stack](/founder-playbook/build/) describes.

**Month three.** 138 recorded hours. None-of-the-above is down to 51. Direction and money are up to 11 combined. He has not become less busy; he has become busy with different things, and the difference is that the four duties are now attended.

**What he got wrong.** He also tried to delegate the first-call sales conversations, to a salesperson hired two months earlier, and took those back after five weeks. The deals were not closing, and the reason was that the motion was not yet describable — exactly the failure [founder-led sales](/founder-playbook/sell/) warns about. Delegating the four duties too early is a different mistake from delegating comfort work too late, and he made both in the same quarter.

## Checklist

The audit:

- [ ] Last month's calendar, every hour sorted: direction, money, top layer, last decision, selling, none of the above.
- [ ] The none-of-the-above total written down as a number.
- [ ] The single largest category named, and an honest answer to whether it is my old job or my refuge.
- [ ] The duties that got zero hours identified. Usually direction and money.

Delegation:

- [ ] Every candidate task classified: recoverable or not, information local or not.
- [ ] Recoverable-and-local work handed over this week, with worse output accepted for a quarter.
- [ ] Context transfer treated as its own scheduled task where I am the only one who knows something.
- [ ] A written list of what stays with me and why, so it is a decision rather than a default.

Standing practice:

- [ ] Runway known to the week, refreshed weekly, by me.
- [ ] Direction on one page, including what we are not doing, read aloud to the company.
- [ ] Hard-to-reverse decisions recorded in three sentences: what, why, what would change my mind.
- [ ] A few customer conversations a month, permanently, regardless of who else sells.
- [ ] A recurring monthly block to repeat this audit, because it decays.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** Work stops when you are away for three days.
**Cause:** You are the bottleneck. Decisions are queueing rather than being made.
**Fix:** Name five decision types and the person who now owns each. Publish the list. Resist being consulted on them for one month.

**Symptom:** You end the week unable to say what you did.
**Cause:** The week was assembled from urgency rather than from the four duties.
**Fix:** The audit. Then one pre-booked block per duty, placed before the week fills.

**Symptom:** You are doing the thing you are best at, and it is not on the list of four.
**Cause:** Refuge work. The most comfortable and most expensive failure.
**Fix:** Delegate it on the reversibility test, keep the two genuinely irreversible slices, and expect the dip.

**Symptom:** You delegated something and took it back.
**Cause:** Either you reclaimed it during the expected dip, or you delegated one of the four duties, which is a different and worse mistake.
**Fix:** Decide which it was. If the dip, hand it back with an agreed review date. If a duty, keep it and say so.

**Symptom:** Different parts of the company are optimising for different things.
**Cause:** Direction is not written or not repeated, so each function invented a coherent local version.
**Fix:** One page, including the refusals, said out loud until you are bored of it. Boredom is the leading indicator of it working.

**Symptom:** The runway number surprises you.
**Cause:** Money is being held by someone else, so you are making decisions inside a constraint you cannot see.
**Fix:** Thirty minutes a week, with the spreadsheet open, by you. Non-negotiable.

**Symptom:** Everything feels equally weighty and nothing moves quickly.
**Cause:** No reversibility sort. Reversible decisions are getting irreversible-decision treatment.
**Fix:** Ask "can we undo this in a month?" before any deliberation. If yes, decide now, alone.
