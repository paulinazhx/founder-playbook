## TL;DR

- Start at **nine to twelve months of runway**. A raise takes three to six months, and your remaining runway is visible to everyone you meet. Starting at four months converts a negotiation into a rescue and the terms show it.
- **Batch first meetings into two to three weeks.** Sequential meetings mean every investor knows nobody else is deciding, which removes the only leverage you have.
- Compute your own dilution rather than trusting a benchmark. For a post-money valuation cap SAFE, [Y Combinator states the rule plainly](https://www.ycombinator.com/documents): ownership sold equals the investment divided by the cap. **Those fractions add across every note you have issued.**
- Build the **data room before the first meeting**. Diligence requests arrive exactly when interest peaks, and a two-week scramble cools a deal that was closing.
- The strongest thing an investor can read is **twelve months of honest monthly updates**, including the bad months. It cannot be manufactured during a raise, which is why it is worth so much.
- If nobody says yes, find the **repeated reason**. Three investors naming the same gap is more useful information than a round would have been.

## Who this is for

You are raising a first institutional round — pre-seed or seed — for the first time. You have probably been told to "just start talking to investors" and have discovered that this is not instructions.

Skip this if you are raising a Series A with a board and a CFO; the process is professionalised and your existing investors will run much of it. Skip it too if your real question is whether to raise at all, which is a strategy question — the short version is that revenue is the only form of funding that does not dilute you, and [pricing](/founder-playbook/sell/pricing/) is frequently a faster route to runway than a round.

## The problem

Founders raise badly for a structural reason, not a skill reason: **fundraising is the one activity where your weakness is the input to your price.**

Everywhere else, being short of money is a private fact. In a raise it is a term. An investor meeting a founder with four months of runway is not evaluating the same opportunity as one meeting a founder with twelve, even if the company is identical. They know you cannot walk away. The instrument, the cap, the control terms and the speed all move accordingly, and nothing about the pitch recovers it.

This produces the central mistake, which is one of **timing rather than technique**. Founders raise when they need money. The need is what reduces the outcome. Everything else in this guide is downstream of starting early.

The second mistake is treating a raise as a sequence of conversations rather than a process with a shape. Meeting investors one at a time, as introductions arrive, means every investor knows that no decision is imminent, so none of them has a reason to decide. Investors are not being difficult; waiting is simply free for them and expensive for you. A batched process makes waiting cost something, and that is the entire mechanism.

The third is mistaking the artifact for the work. Founders spend three weeks on a deck. The deck gets you a meeting; it does not get you money. What gets you money is some combination of the team, something specific that is working, and evidence that you do what you say — and the third of those is accumulated over months, not assembled in a week.

There is also a thing worth saying plainly about the numbers. You will go looking for benchmarks — median round size, median valuation, median dilution — and you will find figures in abundance. Treat them carefully. Many circulating numbers are restatements of restatements, and the primary sources that do publish real benchmarks, such as Carta's quarterly reports drawn from their own cap-table data, update them every quarter and often block automated access. Look them up yourself, from the source, at the time you need them. This guide deliberately does not quote a median, because a median quoted in October and read in March is worse than no median at all.

## What investors actually read

Five artifacts. In rough order of what decides the outcome.

| Artifact | What it is | What it decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The team's track record** | What you have done, and specifically what you have done in this domain. | Whether the meeting happens. Mostly fixed, mostly not something you can change this quarter. |
| **The one thing that is working** | A specific, defensible claim with a number and a definition. Not a projection. | Most of the first meeting. "Revenue grew" decides nothing; "11 of our 14 customers renewed and the three that did not were all single-location" decides a lot. |
| **The metrics, with definitions** | A short set of numbers, each defined. What counts as a customer, as active, as revenue. | Diligence, and your credibility. An undefined metric that shifts between the deck and the data room is read as either sloppiness or spin, and the two cost the same. |
| **The deck** | 10 to 15 slides. Problem, what you do, what is working, market, team, what the money is for. | Whether the first meeting happens, and nothing else. |
| **The update history** | Monthly investor updates going back as far as you have them. | Trust, which is most of what a seed investment is. See [investor updates](/founder-playbook/run/investor-updates/). |

The fifth row is the one founders underweight, and it is the only one that is cheap to build and impossible to fake. A year of monthly updates — including the two bad months, described as bad — tells an investor what you are like to be in business with, in a way no meeting can. If you are twelve months from raising, start sending them now, to anyone who has expressed interest. It is the highest-return fundraising activity available to you today and it does not feel like fundraising.

## The process

**Weeks −4 to 0: prepare.**

Build the list. Forty to eighty named investors, each with a reason: they invest at your stage, in your sector, with cheque sizes that fit, and they are not already in a direct competitor. Rank into three tiers. Beside each, the warmest route to an introduction.

Build the data room. Cap table, financial model, incorporation documents, customer contracts, key employment agreements, the metrics with their definitions, and anything with a signature on it. This takes about a week and it is the difference between answering a diligence request in a day and in a fortnight. The requests arrive at peak interest; a fortnight is enough for that interest to cool.

Write the deck and the two-paragraph forwardable email. The email matters more than the deck, because introductions happen by forwarding.

Rehearse on tier three. Take four or five meetings with investors you do not expect to lead. You will be bad in the first two. Being bad in front of your top choice is an avoidable cost.

**Weeks 1 to 3: batched first meetings.**

Every tier-one and tier-two first meeting inside a three-week window. Fifteen to thirty meetings. It is exhausting and it is the whole strategy: you want interest arriving simultaneously so that a decision has a reason to happen now.

Do not answer "how much are you raising and at what price" with a precise number in week one if you can avoid it. "We're raising around £1.5m and talking to a few funds about leading" is enough. The price benefits from being discovered by the process rather than announced into it.

**Weeks 3 to 8: second meetings and diligence.**

Deeper dives, partner meetings, customer references, the data room. Keep every interested party roughly in step and tell them the truth about timing: "we're planning to make a decision in the week of the 14th." This is not a bluff if it is true, and it is the forcing function the whole batched structure exists to create.

**Weeks 6 to 12: terms, documents, money.**

A lead sets the price, or you fill the round on SAFEs without one. Then legal, which takes two to six weeks depending on the instrument and how clean your cap table is.

Three to six months, start to finish, is the honest plan. Which is the arithmetic behind starting at nine to twelve months of runway: a six-month raise from a nine-month position leaves three months of margin, and a six-month raise from a five-month position is a crisis in week nine.

## The instrument

Two real choices at this stage.

**A post-money SAFE.** No price is set, no lead is required, each investor signs the same short document, and money arrives as it is signed. Y Combinator publishes the standard forms and standardised on post-money versions in 2018. The current US variants are **"SAFE: Valuation Cap, no Discount"**, **"SAFE: Discount, no Valuation Cap"**, and **"SAFE: 'Uncapped MFN' (no Valuation Cap, no Discount)"**, with an optional **Pro Rata Side Letter**, plus valuation-cap versions for Canada, the Cayman Islands and Singapore. YC's own guidance is to keep the SAFE unmodified and put any negotiated rights in a side letter — advice worth following, because a modified SAFE loses the one thing it is for, which is that nobody has to read it.

**A priced equity round.** A lead negotiates a valuation, you issue actual shares, there are real documents, often a board seat and protective provisions. More expensive, slower, and cleaner: everyone knows exactly what they own, immediately.

The arithmetic you must do yourself, and it is simple. For a post-money valuation cap SAFE, **the ownership sold equals the investment divided by the cap**. YC's own illustration: $500,000 on a $6.7 million post-money cap is about 7.5%.

The failure mode specific to SAFEs is **stacking**. Each individual note feels small. They convert together. A founder who has issued five notes at three different caps over eighteen months frequently cannot say what they will own after conversion, and finds out during a priced round, at the worst possible moment to be surprised. Maintain a single sheet with every instrument, its amount, its cap or discount, and its implied percentage. Update it the day each one is signed. This takes four minutes and prevents the most common unforced error at this stage.

## Which terms matter

**Matter a lot:** the valuation cap or price, because it sets dilution. Pro rata rights, because they shape who can fund you next time. Board composition in a priced round. Liquidation preference beyond a plain 1x non-participating. Anything granting a veto over future financings.

**Matter less than founders think:** small differences in cap — the difference between a £6m and a £7m cap is real and smaller than the difference between raising and not raising. Information rights, which you should be meeting anyway. Most side letters.

**Watch for:** full ratchet anti-dilution, participating preferred, multiple liquidation preferences, a mandatory redemption right, or anything that lets one investor block a future round. These are uncommon at seed and are worth a lawyer's hour to check for rather than assuming.

Get a lawyer who does this every week. A generalist will take longer, miss things, and cost more.

## If nobody says yes

Run the same analysis you would on lost sales deals, because it is the same problem: sort the declines by reason.

**"Too early."** Sometimes a genuine stage mismatch, often a polite no. The test is whether they name a specific milestone that would change it. A specific milestone is real; "come back when there's more traction" is not.

**A repeated specific gap.** Three or more investors naming the same missing thing — a metric, a hire, a category of evidence — is the most valuable output of a failed raise. It is a real finding about your company, obtained for free.

**"We don't believe the market is big enough."** Usually unfixable in this raise and worth taking seriously rather than arguing with, because it is a judgement about the thing you cannot change quickly.

And the option that deserves more respect than it gets: **get to default alive on revenue**, which [extending runway](/founder-playbook/run/runway/) sets out as a sequence rather than an aspiration. Cut costs, raise prices, and reach the point where the company funds itself. That converts fundraising from a necessity into an option, which is a strictly better position to raise from — and the arithmetic of failure is unambiguous about why. [CB Insights' March 2026 analysis of 431 failed VC-backed companies](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/) found **70%** ran out of capital, while noting that capital exhaustion "is almost always the final cause of death, not the root problem."

## Worked example

*A hypothetical composite. Numbers are invented to be plausible; no company described here exists.*

Two founders, a B2B product, £31,000 of monthly recurring revenue growing about 7% a month, eleven months of runway. They have previously raised £400,000 across three SAFEs over two years: £150,000 at a £4m post-money cap, £150,000 at a £6m cap, and £100,000 at an £8m cap.

**The stacking check, first.** Before anything else they compute what they have already sold, using investment divided by cap on each:

- £150,000 ÷ £4m = **3.75%**
- £150,000 ÷ £6m = **2.50%**
- £100,000 ÷ £8m = **1.25%**

Total: **7.5%** already committed, before this round and before any option pool. Neither founder had computed this; both had assumed "about 5%." The seven-and-a-half is not a disaster, and discovering it during a priced round would have been an unpleasant surprise in a conversation where surprises are expensive.

**Preparation, four weeks.** List of 61 funds, each with a stage, sector and cheque-size reason, ranked in three tiers, with the warmest introduction route beside each — 23 have one. Data room assembled, including a one-page definitions sheet, because their "active customer" number differed between two earlier updates and they want one definition everywhere from now on. Five rehearsal meetings with tier three; the first two go badly, in ways that are specific and fixable.

**Weeks 1 to 3.** 26 first meetings. Exhausting, and the simultaneity does its work: by the end of week three they have 7 second meetings booked and 2 funds asking about leading.

**Weeks 4 to 7.** Diligence. Four customer reference calls. One fund drops out over market size, which the founders record and do not argue with. The data room is requested by three funds; because it already existed, each request is answered within a day.

They tell everyone, truthfully, that they intend to decide in the week of the 9th.

**Week 8.** Two term sheets. One at a £14m post-money cap on SAFEs with no lead; one priced at £12m pre-money with a board seat and a 1x non-participating preference. They take the priced round at the lower headline number, for two reasons: the cap table gets clean after eighteen months of accumulating notes, and the lead's pro rata commitment materially improves the next round's odds. The 2m difference in headline valuation costs them roughly 1.5% more dilution; they judge the clean cap table and the lead worth it.

**Money in the bank: week 13.** Just over three months from the first meeting, which is at the fast end of the honest range, and it was fast because the data room existed and the metrics had definitions.

**What they got wrong.** They had not sent investor updates for the previous nine months — they had sent two, both after good quarters. Two of their existing SAFE holders had to be re-briefed from scratch during the raise, which took two calls each and visibly affected one of them's willingness to participate. The thing that would have fixed it costs about forty minutes a month and had to be forgone precisely when it would have paid.

## Checklist

Before the first meeting:

- [ ] Nine to twelve months of runway remaining. If fewer, cut costs first and raise from a stronger position.
- [ ] Every existing instrument on one sheet with its amount, cap or discount, and implied percentage — and the total computed.
- [ ] 40 to 80 named investors, each with a stage, sector and cheque-size reason, in three tiers.
- [ ] Warmest introduction route identified for each.
- [ ] Data room assembled: cap table, model, incorporation documents, contracts, metrics definitions.
- [ ] One definitions sheet, so every number means the same thing everywhere.
- [ ] 10 to 15 slide deck and a two-paragraph forwardable email.
- [ ] Four or five rehearsal meetings with tier three, completed.

During:

- [ ] Every tier-one and tier-two first meeting inside a three-week window.
- [ ] A true decision date communicated to everyone.
- [ ] Interested parties kept roughly in step rather than sequenced.
- [ ] Diligence requests answered within 48 hours.
- [ ] Declines logged with their stated reason.

Before signing:

- [ ] Dilution computed for this instrument — investment divided by cap — and added to the existing total.
- [ ] A specialist lawyer has read it.
- [ ] Checked for full ratchet, participating preferred, multiple preference, mandatory redemption, and financing vetoes.
- [ ] SAFEs left unmodified, with any negotiated rights in a side letter.
- [ ] Pro rata rights understood, including what they commit you to next round.

## Failure modes

**Symptom:** Every investor is interested and nobody decides.
**Cause:** Sequential meetings. Waiting costs them nothing.
**Fix:** Batch the remainder, set a real decision date, and tell everyone the same date.

**Symptom:** Terms are worse than peers with comparable companies.
**Cause:** You started too late and it is visible.
**Fix:** If you can still cut to extend runway, do it and restart from strength. Four months of runway is a term, not a secret.

**Symptom:** Diligence stalls for two weeks and the energy drains out of the deal.
**Cause:** No data room, so each request becomes a project.
**Fix:** Assemble it in a week. The ordering matters: it is only cheap before the raise.

**Symptom:** An investor finds a discrepancy in your numbers.
**Cause:** No single definition of a metric, so the deck and the data room disagree.
**Fix:** One definitions sheet, applied retroactively to everything already sent, with a short note explaining the correction. Correcting it yourself costs far less than it being found.

**Symptom:** You cannot say what you will own after the SAFEs convert.
**Cause:** Stacking, unmodelled.
**Fix:** The one sheet, today. Investment divided by cap for each, summed.

**Symptom:** Existing investors are lukewarm about participating.
**Cause:** They have not heard from you except when you need something.
**Fix:** Nothing fast. Start monthly updates now so the next raise is different.

**Symptom:** Three investors each named a different reason and you have changed the pitch three times.
**Cause:** Treating individual feedback as signal. One investor's reason is noise.
**Fix:** Change nothing until you hear the same reason three times. Then it is a finding.
